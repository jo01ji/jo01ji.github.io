Planar to Widmanstätten Ferrite
Ferrite morphology · Fe–C and Fe–C–Mn · competing explanations

From a planar ferrite film to Widmanstätten sideplates

The same steel can grow ferrite as a smooth film along a prior-austenite grain boundary, or as sharp sideplates that shoot into the grain. Intermediate shapes are rarely seen, and there is no agreed explanation for which path a given boundary takes. This lesson builds the length scales first, then lays out four explanations side by side without choosing between them.

1 · Three products at one boundary

Classify by shape and by what the ferrite is rooted on

Ws, the Widmanstätten-start temperature

The temperature below which sideplates become detectable. It is the best documented descriptor, but it shifts with prior-austenite grain size and with the detection criterion, and it says nothing about whether the plates are primary or secondary.

Why “transition” needs care

A colony attached to an allotriomorph does not prove that one ferrite crystal changed shape. It could be continuous growth, new plates nucleated on the film, or plates that grew separately and later touched. “Local morphology selection” is the neutral name for the problem.

Plate-like is not one thing

Widmanstätten and bainitic ferrite cannot be told apart from shape or surface relief alone. Thermal path, carbides, carbon partitioning and crystallography are needed, and unresolved cases are best labelled ambiguous plate-like ferrite.

2 · The length scales, drawn to scale

A film’s carbon field grows with its thickness. A tip’s field stays the size of the tip.

Ferrite holds almost no carbon, so every bit of ferrite that grows pushes carbon into the austenite ahead of it. Growth is limited by how fast that carbon diffuses away. The two geometries handle this very differently, and the slider below lets you watch the gap open up.

Front velocity against time

Planar film (allotriomorph)

V = (D/W)·(C₁γ−C₁₀)² / [(C₁₀−C₁α)(C₁γ−C₁α)]

This is the Zener-type relation in the Bréchet note. The carbon field width δ is proportional to W, so the gradient that drains carbon gets shallower as the film thickens. Integrating gives parabolic growth, W ∝ √(Dt), and a velocity that falls as 1/√t.

Sideplate tip (needle)

V ≈ (D/2ρ)·(C₁γ−C₁₀)/(C₁₀−C₁α)·(1 − ρc/ρ)

This is the Zener–Hillert estimate for plate lengthening. Carbon escapes sideways around a sharp tip, so the field ahead of it is only about the size of ρ and does not grow with time. Lengthening is linear in time. Curvature costs driving force, which is the (1 − ρc/ρ) term, and the velocity peaks at ρ = 2ρc.

Move the time slider from 0.01 s to 30 s. The film’s field δ grows in step with W, from tens of nanometres to microns, while the tip field D/V stays at tens of nanometres. Any explanation of the transition has to deal with this mismatch, and the Bréchet note in section 6 builds directly on it.

3 · Key concept: solute drag by Mn

Mn sits in the moving interface and pulls back on it, most strongly at intermediate speeds

Mn concentrationferrite sideaustenite side

Drag pressure P(V) (Cahn-type)

Why the curve has a peak

When the interface is slow, Mn segregated to it keeps up and the profile stays almost symmetric, so the net pull is small. At intermediate speed the Mn cloud lags behind and pulls hard. When the interface is fast, Mn cannot follow at all and the drag falls away. Cahn described this for grain boundaries and Purdy and Bréchet applied it to ferrite growth.

Paraequilibrium and NPLE

In Fe–C–Mn, carbon diffuses about a million times faster than Mn. If Mn does not redistribute at all, the interface is at paraequilibrium. Local equilibrium with negligible partitioning (NPLE) keeps a tiny Mn spike in front of the interface. The fast branch of the drag curve tends toward paraequilibrium.

Force balance

Purdy and Bréchet find the interface velocity where chemical driving force equals drag plus intrinsic friction, ΔG/Vm = P(V) + V/M. Hillert later showed that a complete balance also needs the dissipation in the solute spike in the parent austenite, which their comparison left out.

4 · Explanation A continuous growth

Interface instability: bumps on the film outgrow the flat parts

Townsend and Kirkaldy studied Fe–C and treated secondary sideplates as growth from an existing ferrite/austenite interface. A bump that sticks out sees a steeper carbon gradient, so it drains carbon faster and grows faster. Curvature (capillarity) pushes back, hardest on short wavelengths. The Mullins–Sekerka analysis gives a fastest-growing wavelength, and Sekerka noted that their sideplate spacings were close to it.

Sketch form: σ(λ) = V(2π/λ)[1 − (λs/λ)²] with λs = 2π√(d₀·2D/V), using ρc as the capillary length d₀. Anisotropy, facets and interface kinetics are left out.

What supports it

  • Sideplate group spacing in Fe–C was reasonably consistent with the predicted spacing of interface protuberances (Townsend and Kirkaldy; Sekerka).
  • Interface history matters. Ferrite/austenite interfaces equilibrated for a long time near A3 developed fewer instabilities than fresh ones.
  • Phase-field models (Loginova, Yamanaka, Yan) start from a perturbed allotriomorph and grow plates from it, but only when interfacial-energy anisotropy is high.

What it leaves open

  • It explains amplification, not how the first sideplate began.
  • It cannot produce primary sideplates, which grow from a bare grain boundary with no film to perturb.
  • Intermediate wavy shapes are rarely seen, which is awkward for a continuous process.
  • A continuous protrusion should keep the orientation of the film. That needs crystallographic checking.
5 · Explanation B discrete events

Sympathetic nucleation: new plates nucleate on ferrite that is already there

In Aaronson’s description, a new crystal of the same phase nucleates on an existing ferrite/austenite interface. The new plate usually differs from its substrate by a small misorientation, separated by a low-angle boundary. Plates can nucleate edge-to-face on an allotriomorph or a grain boundary, and face-to-face on the broad face of an existing plate. Step through a colony below, then switch the view.

What supports it

  • Phelan and Dippenaar watched sideplates develop from stepped or faceted allotriomorphs by in situ confocal microscopy, then found low-angle misorientation between allotriomorph and colony by EBSD.
  • Phelan et al. recorded stepwise changes in growth direction and orientation with low-angle sub-boundaries.
  • Cheng et al. saw edge-to-face nucleation from boundaries and allotriomorphs, and face-to-face nucleation on plate broad faces, in Fe–C–Mn.
  • Cao’s 3D FIB/SEM-EBSD found small-angle boundaries between sideplates, and a small orientation change near the allotriomorph/sideplate contact.
  • Garzon (Fe–Ni–C, internal report): sideplates formed faster from allotriomorph-preconditioned austenite, and the colonies looked more like repeated nucleation and coalescence than one travelling instability. This rests on optical metallography only, without crystallography.

What it leaves open

  • It names where plates start, but not what makes a site on the film favourable at one moment and not another.
  • It needs a nucleation criterion that existing growth models do not supply.
  • Coalescence and impingement can erase the evidence, so a final micrograph cannot prove it.
  • It does not by itself explain the narrow temperature window or the sharpness of Ws.
6 · Explanation C growth-branch change discussion note, unpublished

Kinetic branches: as the film thickens, its growth solution can be lost

The Bréchet note starts from the Purdy–Bréchet force balance. For a trial velocity V, carbon diffusion sets the interface carbon content and so the driving force ΔG(V). The operating velocity is where ΔG(V) meets the resistance P(V) + V/M. The film’s curve depends on W, while the needle’s depends on ρc. As W grows, the film’s curve retreats to low velocities. With strong solute drag there is a velocity gap in the middle that no stable solution can sit in. This is a hypothesis about ferrite that already exists, and the note does not claim it is quantitative.

The note’s three outcomes

  • Weak drag, where intrinsic mobility dominates: at W* the plate gives way to the needle, plate → needle, with no stasis.
  • Strong drag, needle reachable: the plate drops to the slow branch while a needle runs on the fast branch, plate → needle + plate.
  • Strong drag, needle not reachable: growth stalls, plate → stasis.

The needle curve here uses the note’s prefactor 2D/ρc and halves ΔG for the capillary cost at ρ = 2ρc. The note does not derive this prefactor or W*, so treat the numbers as an illustration of the construction rather than predictions.

What it leaves open

  • Losing the plate solution does not prove that a needle must follow. The work needed to create a tip is not included.
  • It does not say whether the needle grows continuously out of the film or nucleates sympathetically. Hillert notes that a jump to a separated fast branch needs a fluctuation or dynamic nucleation.
  • The [V₁, V₂] gap is labelled unstable in the sketch, but no dynamic stability analysis has been done.
  • It applies to ferrite that already exists, so it does not address primary sideplates.
  • W is used both as the film thickness and as the diffusion-field width. Those need to be checked against the measured carbon field.
7 · Explanation D model-supplied selection

9 · Other key concepts
Reconstructive and displacive

Allotriomorphs are usually treated as diffusional and reconstructive. Widmanstätten and bainitic ferrite are diffusional-displacive: carbon diffuses, but the iron lattice changes by a coordinated shear with a surface relief and a habit plane.

Capillary length ρc

The tip radius at which interface curvature uses up the whole driving force. Below ρc a tip cannot grow. It is roughly σVm/ΔG, so it shrinks as undercooling rises.

Péclet number

Vρ/2D compares the tip size with its diffusion length. A value near one means the carbon field ahead of the tip is about as big as the tip itself.

Ledges and disconnections

Partly coherent ferrite/austenite boundaries advance by steps sweeping across them. They describe how a broad face moves and how a plate and allotriomorph may join, but they do not locate the starting site.

Soft impingement

Carbon fields of neighbouring ferrite overlap before the crystals touch. The austenite between them is already enriched, which slows both and can suppress candidate tips.

Hard impingement and coalescence

Neighbouring plates meet. Where their orientations are close, the boundary between them can vanish, so separate events look like one crystal.

Interfacial-energy anisotropy

The ferrite/austenite energy depends on boundary orientation, low for coherent broad faces near the orientation relationship and high elsewhere. Phase-field models need it high to make plates.

Grain size and Ws

Krahe, Kinsman and Aaronson showed the apparent Ws rises with prior-austenite grain size. Selection depends on local boundary conditions, not only chemistry and temperature.

Wrinkled ferrite

A non-planar ferrite interface reported in Fe–C–Mo. Purdy and Bréchet compared their predicted unstable conditions with it. Mo is a strong drag element, which is why an Fe–C–Mo alloy would test the stasis branch directly.
