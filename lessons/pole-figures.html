Part 1

How a crystal plane becomes a dot

Put one grain at the centre of an imaginary sphere. Every plane in a family, say the four {111} planes, has a normal. Where each normal pierces the sphere is its pole.

Draw a line from the pole to the south pole of the sphere. Where that line crosses the equatorial plane is the dot you see on the pole figure. That is the stereographic projection. The equatorial plane is the sheet surface, so the centre of the figure is the sheet normal ND, and the outer circle is everything lying in the sheet, with the rolling direction RD and transverse direction TD marked on it.

Two consequences matter for reading. Distance from the centre is the tilt angle α from ND, but it is not linear: r = tan(α/2), so the outer 30° of tilt is squashed into a narrower band than the inner 30°. And because a plane normal has no front or back, a pole that would land on the lower hemisphere is flipped to its opposite end, which is always on the upper one.

Part 2

A reading routine that works for any cubic pole figure

  1. Check the frame before the dots

    Find RD, TD and ND, the plane family ({111}, {110}, {200}…), and the phase. Conventions vary: some labs put RD at the top, others to the right. Intensities are in multiples of random density (mrd), where 1 means “as many poles here as a random sample would have”.

  2. Know how many poles one grain makes

    One grain gives 3 poles on a {100} figure, 6 on {110}, 4 on {111} and 12 on {112}. If you count 4 blobs on a {111} figure, that can be one strong orientation. If you count 8, look for two, or for sample symmetry making copies.

  3. Read the centre: which plane lies flat in the sheet

    A pole at the centre means that plane is parallel to the sheet surface, so ND is parallel to its normal. Cube texture puts a {100} pole at the centre. The γ-fibre in ferrite puts a {111} pole there.

  4. Read the rim at RD: which direction was rolled

    A pole sitting on the outer circle at RD tells you a crystal direction lies along RD. Goss has a {100} pole on RD. Copper has a {111} pole on RD. The α-fibre in ferrite has a {110} pole on RD.

  5. Use the other poles as a check

    Once you have guessed {hkl}⟨uvw⟩ from the centre and RD, the remaining poles must sit at the right angles, for example 54.7° between ⟨100⟩ and ⟨111⟩, or 60° and 90° between ⟨110⟩ poles. The table on the bench lists α and β for every pole so you can check the guess.

  6. Expect copies from sample symmetry

    A rolled sheet looks the same after a 180° turn about RD, TD or ND. So one ideal component like copper or S shows up two or four times, mirrored about RD and TD. Tick “Rolled-sheet symmetry” and watch a single S orientation become four.

  7. Read blobs and bands, not points

    Real samples have spread. A texture component becomes a blob whose width is the spread; a fibre (all orientations sharing one axis) becomes a ring or a band. One pole figure cannot always separate overlapping components; that is what the orientation distribution function (ODF) is for.

Part 3

FCC versus BCC: is the reading different?

No. The reading routine and the geometry are identical, because austenite and ferrite are both cubic. A grain with a given orientation puts its {100}, {110} and {111} poles in exactly the same places whether the atoms are arranged FCC or BCC. Switch the phase on the bench with any orientation: the dots do not move, only the colour and the unit cell change. What differs is what you measure and what you expect to find.
