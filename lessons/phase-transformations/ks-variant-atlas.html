The 24 Kurdjumov–Sachs variants

{111}γ ∥ {011}α  ·  ⟨101⟩γ ∥ ⟨111⟩α

One austenite (fcc) grain can transform into 24 martensite (bcc) orientations that all satisfy the same KS relationship. Each one puts a close-packed {011}α plane on one of the four {111}γ planes and lines up a close-packed ⟨111⟩α row with a ⟨110⟩γ row inside it. Pick a variant anywhere on the page and every view follows, including the literature sections further down.

Packets

The six variants sharing one {111}γ plane form a packet in lath martensite. They are the same habit plane, rotated in-plane by the three ⟨110⟩γ rows.

Blocks and sub-blocks

Within a packet, pairs 10.53° apart (V1/V4, V2/V5, V3/V6) share a Bain axis and form a block of two sub-blocks. Each pair sits either side of one Nishiyama–Wassermann orientation, 5.26° from each: use the twist slider to walk from one to the other.

Bain groups

Every variant is close to one of the three Bain orientations, compressed along [100]γ, [010]γ or [001]γ. Each Bain group holds 8 variants, two from each plane.

Section 1Why KS, and why it never fits exactly

The {111}γ and {011}α planes do not match atom for atom. KS is the best partial match: one close-packed row lines up almost exactly, and the rest of the misfit is taken up by steps, dislocations and a small extra rotation.

How big the misfit is

The readouts under the plane view give the numbers for the lattice parameters you set. At 1000 K and 1 at.% C (Kop et al. 2001, Table I):

  • Along the shared row (⟨110⟩γ ∥ ⟨111⟩α): 2.573 Å vs 2.508 Å, about 2.5% short in α.
  • Across the rows: 2.228 Å in (111)γ vs 2.365 Å in (011)α, about 6% long in α.
  • Angle between rows: 60° in the fcc plane, 70.53° in the bcc plane. Once one row is matched the next is 10.5° off.

KS and Nishiyama–Wassermann (NW) are the two ways of splitting that 10.5°. KS puts it all on one side and matches a close-packed row exactly. NW splits it 5.26° each side and matches ⟨100⟩α with a ⟨110⟩γ instead. Lath martensite sits between the two, near the Greninger–Troiano (GT) orientation (Howe et al., p. 814).

The twist slider, read against the papers

Howe, Pond and Hirth treat the (111)γ/(011)α terrace as coherent and let defect networks take up the misfit. They walk the twist from NW (0°) through GT (2.5°) to KS (5.26°) and find the GT network closest to what TEM shows in lath martensite (pp. 818–819). The slider above runs the same path for the selected variant, then carries on to the KS partner on the other side of NW.

Cottura et al. build the KS eigenstrain as the Bain strain plus a 5.26° rotation that aligns the close-packed directions. In their phase-field model a plate with that strain grows at constant speed along its invariant line (p. 208). So KS is what you get when the Bain strain is rotated until a close-packed row is undistorted.

Why the habit plane is {557}γ, not {111}γ

If the interface were a flat (111)γ terrace, laths would lie on {111}γ. They don't: lath martensite grows on planes near {557}γ, about 9.4° away. In the topological model the interface is (111)γ terraces broken by disconnections, defects that are both a dislocation and a step of height h. A regular array of steps spaced d apart tilts the average interface by ψ = tan⁻¹(h/d) (Howe et al., p. 818). For their GT network ψ = 9.18°, giving a habit plane (0.505 0.700 0.505)γ, which is (575)γ to within a fraction of a degree.

The tilt axis is the step line, which lies along the shared close-packed row. That is why the {557}γ plane of each variant contains its ⟨110⟩γ row: V1 and V2 (row [101]γ) share (575)γ, V3 and V4 share (755)γ, V5 and V6 share (557)γ. Tick "Show the lath habit plane" in the 3D view to see it.

Beyond the papersAssigning one {557}γ to each variant is my geometry: it is the only {557}γ plane within 10° of the variant's {111}γ that contains its parallel ⟨110⟩γ. It agrees with Nyyssönen et al.'s measured habit (0.49, 0.70, 0.52)γ and long axis (−0.71, −0.03, 0.70)γ for their standard variant, which is V1 here (section 3).

Section 2KS in diffusional transformations

KS is not only a martensite relationship. Widmanstätten ferrite, bainite and even grain-boundary allotriomorphs carry it on at least one side. Where it holds, the interface is semi-coherent, slow and full of ledges; where it doesn't, the interface is incoherent and mobile. Almost every topic downstream of nucleation (sympathetic nucleation, interface mobility, solute drag, plate shape) depends on which kind of interface you are looking at.

Two sides of one crystal

A ferrite crystal that nucleates on an austenite grain boundary usually has an orientation relationship with only one of the two grains. Aaronson and Wells named the two interfaces: the boundary with the grain it nucleated in is the oriented-lattices boundary, the boundary with the other grain the unoriented-lattices boundary. In steel the oriented side is the KS (sometimes NW) side.

Near Ae3 the oriented side is flat, highly coherent and hard to move, so the allotriomorph thickens mainly into the other grain through the curved, incoherent unoriented boundary (Dong 2021, p. 5).

Well below Ae3 the driving force is large enough to pay for the transformation strain, and growth goes through the KS interface alone: the ferrite turns acicular or plate-like and grows as Widmanstätten or bainitic ferrite into the oriented grain (Dong 2021, p. 6). This is the ferrite that grows into the austenite on the KS side.

Sympathetic nucleation follows the same split. Aaronson and Wells found it most often on intragranular plates, occasionally on oriented-lattices boundaries, and rarely if ever on unoriented ones. The oriented side has the partially coherent terraces that keep their supersaturation (section 2, Reynolds); the unoriented side keeps moving and has none.

For EBSD: a crystal nucleated on the oriented side is one of the 24 variants of γ1, so the pair finder applies to it. Against γ2 it is just a high-angle misorientation.

Sympathetic nucleation

Why a second crystal is needed. Under KS one ferrite crystal has one {011}α plane parallel to one {111}γ. The four {111}γ planes are 70.53° apart, while {011}α planes are 60° or 90° apart, so a single crystal cannot put plates on two {111}γ. Aaronson and Wells used this (via Dubé and Mehl) to argue that plates on different octahedral planes must be separate crystals with a ferrite/ferrite boundary between them; Aaronson et al. restate it as "branching … is crystallographically impossible".

What the boundary looks like. Sympathetically nucleated crystals usually make a small-angle boundary with their substrate, occasionally a near-coincidence large-angle one. A lower α/α boundary energy lowers the nucleation barrier ΔG* (Fig. 12 of Aaronson et al.). Phelan et al. watched Widmanstätten plates nucleate on allotriomorphs in situ; since the allotriomorph is rarely exactly KS, the plate either picks a variant a degree or two away or pays for a larger boundary.

Where it happens. On the immobile terraces of growth ledges at partially coherent boundaries, which keep their supersaturation after a ledge passes (Shewmon, via Aaronson et al.). Those terraces exist because of a rational orientation relationship such as KS.

The pair finder on the right turns this into numbers for the selected substrate variant. It answers the follow-up in your sympathetic-nucleation reading log: in EBSD, the α/α misorientation between two plates tells you whether they can be the same packet, a sub-block pair, or crystals on different {111}γ.

Interface character and mobility

A grain-boundary allotriomorph usually has KS (sometimes NW) with one austenite grain and no rational relationship with the other. The KS side is flat, highly coherent and slow; the other side is curved, incoherent and grows (Dong 2021, p. 5). Militzer et al. draw the same line between morphologies: Widmanstätten and bainitic ferrite keep an orientation relationship and a shear correspondence with the austenite they grow into, allotriomorphic ferrite does not.

Dong fitted interface velocities against measured interfacial carbon in Fe-0.1C and found the intrinsic mobility of the semi-coherent (Widmanstätten) interface about ten times lower than the incoherent (allotriomorph) one, with an extra ~20 J/mol dissipated by the Widmanstätten transformation strain.

Reynolds et al. give the mechanism: partially coherent broad faces are less mobile than plate edges, and their stationary terraces are where carbides and new ferrite nucleate.

Solute drag and stasis

The solute-drag models in your folder (Purdy & Brechet 1995, Hillert 1999) describe the interface as a region where solute binds and diffuses; they do not depend on the orientation relationship. KS enters through which interface is moving and how strongly it binds solute.

  • Semi-coherent broad faces of Widmanstätten and bainitic plates (rational OR) may segregate less than incoherent interfaces such as the plate tip (Militzer et al., p. 12).
  • The only atomistic numbers in the review come from DFT on a KS bcc/fcc interface; the binding energies are similar to a Σ5 grain boundary in bcc iron (p. 13).
  • In Fe-0.1C-Nb, Dong traces the slow-down of allotriomorphs to Nb segregation at the moving incoherent interface (p. III).
  • In Fe-C-Mo, Reynolds et al. tie their solute-drag-like effect (SDLE) to ledges: a slowed boundary leaves its partially coherent terraces exposed for longer, until sympathetic nucleation can start again on them (p. 1454); the same terraces are the likeliest sites for carbides (p. 1455). Here drag, KS terraces and sympathetic nucleation are one mechanism.

So the question to ask of a drag model is which interface it describes: the incoherent tip and allotriomorph faces, or the KS terraces.

Widmanstätten ferrite and bainite

Militzer et al. call Widmanstätten ferrite "diffusional-displacive": carbon diffusion sets the rate, while the iron lattice changes by disconnection motion, the same defects that set the lath habit in section 1. Its shape change is why stress affects it so much more than it affects allotriomorphs.

Bhadeshia: Widmanstätten ferrite grows as pairs of mutually accommodating variants whose opposing shape changes cancel, storing about 50 J/mol; bainite plates show a single invariant-plane strain and store about 400 J/mol. Ohmori et al.: Widmanstätten ferrite nucleates diffusionally with a KS-type relationship on perturbed allotriomorph interfaces or grain boundaries, then grows displacively.

The plate itself grows along the invariant line of its KS strain, the same direction that becomes the lath long axis (Cottura et al., p. 208; section 3).

Section 3From EBSD back to variants

An EBSD map shows only the bcc orientations. Because each one is a known KS variant of a parent grain, you can work backwards: rebuild the austenite, label every domain with its variant, and rotate the domain shapes into the austenite frame. Nyyssönen et al. use this to recover habit planes and long axes from ordinary 2-D maps.

The workflow

  1. Index the bcc map.
  2. Reconstruct parent austenite grains from the child orientations and the OR.
  3. Label variants: find the symmetry operator, and so the variant number, of each pixel.
  4. Segment variant domains; for lath martensite these are sub-blocks.
  5. Rotate centroid-to-boundary vectors into the austenite frame and fit a shape.

Their standard variant is V1 in Morito's numbering, the same as this page. For quenched 0.7 wt% C martensite they measure a habit plane of (0.49, 0.70, 0.52)γ, about 1° from (575)γ, and a long axis of (−0.71, −0.03, 0.70)γ, which is the [101]γ row that V1 shares with its α crystal. The lath grows along the KS parallel direction.

For your in-situ heating-stage EBSD, the variant table above is the lookup: the misorientation and axis between two neighbouring domains tell you whether they are sub-blocks, blocks or different packets of one parent grain, or not KS-related at all.
