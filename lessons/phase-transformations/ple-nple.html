Paraequilibrium, PLE and NPLE

When ferrite grows from austenite in Fe–C–Mn, carbon diffuses about a million times faster than manganese. Something has to give at the moving interface. This lesson builds the three classic answers (paraequilibrium, local equilibrium with partitioning, and local equilibrium with negligible partitioning) on one isothermal section, follows the growth rates they predict, and then looks at what the experiments say about the ground in between.

1 · Four interface conditions

Each one is a rule about which chemical potentials match across the interface

Full equilibrium needs every element to have the same chemical potential on both sides of the α/γ interface. The other three relax that rule in different ways. The names vary across the literature, so the table lists the ones you will meet in your sources.

ConditionAlso calledEqual across the interfaceMn in the new ferriteWhat sets the rate
Full equilibriumOrthoequilibriumμC, μFe, μMnEquilibrium value, through the alloy’s own tie-lineThe end state, not a growth law
Paraequilibrium (PE)Hultgren 1947, Hillert 1953μC and the Fe+Mn average uFeμFe+uMnμMnSame Mn/Fe ratio as the austenite. No Mn moves at allCarbon diffusion in austenite
NPLELE-NP, LENP, quasi-paraequilibrium (Hillert’s book)μC, μFe, μMn (full local equilibrium)Bulk Mn, but only because a thin Mn spike sits in the austenite ahead of the interfaceCarbon diffusion in austenite
PLELE-P, local equilibrium with partitioningμC, μFe, μMn (full local equilibrium)Less than bulk. Mn is rejected into the austenite over long rangeMn diffusion in austenite, so it is very slow

PE is not a local equilibrium. Fe and Mn have different chemical potentials on the two sides, and only their u-fraction-weighted average matches (Hillert, Eq. 14.13). That is why the PE γ/(α+γ) boundary lies inside the full equilibrium two-phase field, which Hillert states as a general rule. PLE and NPLE are both full local equilibrium; they differ only in which tie-line the interface picks.

2 · The isothermal section

Two fluxes, one velocity: why the interface must choose between PLE and NPLE

If the interface is in full local equilibrium, its two ends sit on a tie-line of the isothermal section. Carbon and Mn must both be carried away from the interface fast enough to keep up with it, so the tie-line has to satisfy two flux balances at once (Coates; Militzer et al., Eq. 7):

v = DC·(∂CC/∂z) / (CCγ − CCα) = DMn·(∂CMn/∂z) / (CMnγ − CMnα)

With DC/DMn around 10⁶ to 10⁷, the two sides can only match in two ways. Either the carbon gradient is almost zero (the tie-line’s γ end sits on the carbon isoactivity line through the alloy, and Mn diffusion sets the pace: PLE), or the Mn gradient is enormous (a thin Mn spike, the ferrite keeps the bulk Mn, and carbon diffusion sets the pace: NPLE). The boundary between the two regions is the envelope of zero partition.

3 · Key concept: Gibbs energy curves

Every interface condition is a tangent construction on the Gibbs energy curves

The tie-lines in section 2 come from the molar Gibbs energy surfaces of ferrite and austenite over carbon and Mn content. Two cuts through those surfaces show all four conditions. Energies are per mole of Fe+Mn atoms (Hillert’s Gm12), so carbon can be added without changing the amount of metal. Each plot subtracts the same straight line from both curves so the curvature is visible. Subtracting a straight line does not move any tangent point or crossing.

Fixing μC turns a plane into a line

A tangent plane to two surfaces is hard to draw. But all four conditions share one carbon potential across the interface. Holding μC fixed and letting each phase take whatever carbon matches it gives the function Φ = Gm12 − uCμC = uFeμFe + uMnμMn (Hillert, Eq. 14.14), which depends on Mn only. On that cut the tangent plane becomes an ordinary common tangent.

Local equilibrium: touch, don’t cross

Full equilibrium, PLE and NPLE are common tangents on the right-hand plot. They differ only in which carbon potential, and so which tangent, the interface uses. Equal slopes mean equal μMn − μFe, and the shared line means equal μFe and μMn separately.

Paraequilibrium: cross, don’t touch

Under PE the two curves only have to meet at the alloy’s Mn content. Equal Φ there is Hillert’s condition uFeμFe+uMnμMn equal on both sides (Eq. 14.13). Their slopes differ, and that difference is the jump in μMn − μFe across a PE interface. Under NPLE the spike removes that jump.

Where the curves cross: T0

Left of the crossing in the carbon cut, ferrite of the same composition as the austenite has lower G, so the change could happen with no diffusion at all. The T0 line joins these crossings at each temperature, and it is the reference used for bainite and massive ferrite.

Why PE and NPLE look alike

Both are carbon-diffusion controlled and both leave the ferrite with the bulk Mn content. They differ only in the carbon content the interface hands to the austenite. PE uses the PE γ boundary. NPLE uses the carbon content at the outer edge of the Mn spike, found by following the carbon isoactivity line from the tie-line’s γ end back to the bulk Mn level. That is why the envelope of zero partition lies below the PE boundary.

Why PLE is so slow

In PLE the same parabolic law applies, but with DMn in place of DC. At these temperatures that cuts the thickness by a factor of √(DC/DMn), around a thousand. Militzer et al. note that little or no ferrite growth is observed in the partitioning regime on industrial time scales, so most applied work has focused on non-partitioned growth.

6 · Between the limits illustrative switch

Fast interfaces look like PE. Slow ones drift toward NPLE

Neither PE nor NPLE fits the data over a wide range of compositions and temperatures. In Fe–C–Ni, Militzer et al. show PE describing the first 30 to 40 s, after which growth slows sharply. Hutchinson and coworkers fitted this with an interface condition that moves from PE at high velocity toward NPLE as the interface slows. The instrument below uses the simplest version of that idea. It compares the interface velocity v with the speed Mn can cross the interface, Dint/δ, and blends the interfacial carbon from PE to NPLE using the interface Péclet number P = vδ/Dint.

7 · Key concept: dissipation at the interface

The modern models find the interface condition from an energy balance

ΔF = v/Mint + SD,    SD = −(Vm/v)∫Ji·∂(μi−μn)/∂z dz
What is being balanced

The chemical driving pressure across the interface ΔF is spent on moving the interface itself (v/Mint) and on diffusion of the substitutional solute in and across the interface (SD, from Hillert and Sundman). Carbon stays in local equilibrium, and its interfacial content adjusts until the two sides balance (Militzer et al., Eqs 8–10).

Why SD peaks at intermediate speed

At v = 0 Mn reaches its equilibrium segregation profile and every chemical potential gradient vanishes. At high v there is no time for Mn to segregate and the flux is zero. In between, Mn interacts with the moving interface and SD passes through a maximum. This is the same shape as the Cahn drag curve in the Widmanstätten lesson.

Two ways to draw the box

Odqvist, Hillert and Ågren take the total driving force and include the spike in the austenite. Hutchinson et al. and Zurob et al. take the driving force across the interface only and limit SD to the interface. Militzer et al. call the two equivalent as long as ΔF and SD are defined over the same region.

Fe–C–Ni against Fe–C–Mo

In Fe–C–Ni most of the dissipation comes from building the Ni spike, because Ni’s chemical potential differs a lot between γ and α under PE. In Fe–C–Mo, PE and NPLE give almost the same Mo potential, but Mo binds strongly to the interface, so SD is large between about 10⁻⁸ and 10⁻⁶ m/s and the interfacial carbon drops sharply in that range.

Stasis

If the dissipation curve has a maximum above the driving force, the interface can jump from the fast branch to the slow branch and ferrite growth nearly stops below both the PE and NPLE fractions. Chen and van der Zwaag model stasis in Fe–C–Mn and Fe–C–Ni this way and find Mn gives more incomplete transformation than Ni. Aaronson’s book ties stasis and the TTT bay to nonequilibrium segregation of Mo and Cr at the boundary.

What is not known

Every version needs the solute binding energy to the interface Eb, the trans-interface diffusivity and the interface mobility, and none of them is well known. Fits to some data sets can be made by adjusting Mint and Eb, which is why Militzer et al. call for better estimates of these parameters.

8 · What the experiments say

Decarburization layers gave the cleanest tests, and most systems sit between the limits

Decarburizing a ternary austenite in wet hydrogen grows a planar ferrite layer from the surface inward, so the interface stays flat and its thickness can be compared directly with PE and NPLE curves. Precipitation experiments measure the ferrite fraction at stasis. Purdy’s 2009 review and the Panahi et al. general model summarise the findings.

9 · Purdy’s list of common misuses

Where PE and NPLE get applied without checking

  • Using “PE” loosely, for example constrained paraequilibrium in martensite tempering.
  • Assuming the ferrite reaches the PE volume fraction after some fixed time such as one hour.
  • Choosing PE or NPLE in advance when setting up a model, instead of letting the interface condition follow from the kinetics.
  • Applying either limit to intercritical annealing of dual-phase, TRIP or TWIP steels, where the conditions differ.

Purdy’s open questions include segregation and C–X co-segregation at the interface, the physical meaning of the spike, diffusion across rather than along the interface, a better model for the TTT bay, and whether sharp-interface concepts still make sense next to diffuse-interface models.

10 · Other key concepts
u-fraction

ui = xi/(xFe+xMn). It counts solute per substitutional atom, so it stays constant for Mn when only carbon moves. Under PE every product lies on the same uMn level as the parent.

Carbon isoactivity line

Compositions of austenite with the same carbon activity. Because Mn lowers the carbon activity, the line rises in carbon as Mn increases. Carbon diffuses down activity gradients, not concentration gradients.

Envelope of zero partition

The locus of alloys for which NPLE is just possible. Below it the NPLE tie-line exists with a positive carbon gradient into the austenite. Also called the zero-partition line.

Spike

The thin enrichment of Mn in the austenite immediately ahead of an NPLE interface. Its width is about DMn/v, so it shrinks as the interface speeds up.

Decarburization experiment

Ferrite grows as a flat layer from a surface held at low carbon activity. It removes nucleation and geometry from the problem and gives clean thickness against √t data.

Transformation stasis

Ferrite growth stops well before the PE or NPLE fraction and resumes only much later, if at all. Often linked to solute drag or carbide precipitation at the interface.
