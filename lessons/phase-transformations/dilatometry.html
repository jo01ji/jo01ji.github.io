Reading a Dilatometer

A dilatometer measures the length of a small cylinder of steel while it is heated, held and cooled under tight control. Every phase in steel packs iron atoms at a slightly different density, so a phase change appears as a kink in an otherwise straight length–temperature line. This lesson builds that signal from lattice parameters, reads phase fractions and start temperatures off it, covers the deformation and tension modules, and ends with the schedule planned for sample-01.

The lever rule reads the fraction as the distance between the two tangents. It assumes the austenite keeps contracting along the line it had before transformation. But the austenite that is left gets richer in carbon, which expands its lattice, so the lever rule misreads the fraction while ferrite is forming.

2 · The instrument

A DIL805 heats the specimen by induction and reads its length through push rods

The specimen is a small cylinder held between two push rods inside an induction coil. A thermocouple is spot-welded to the middle of the specimen, and heating power is controlled from that reading. The push rods carry the change in length out of the hot zone to a displacement transducer. Cooling comes from inert gas blown onto the specimen, in vacuum or inert atmosphere. Because the heated volume is tiny and the coil heats the specimen directly, the specimen can be heated and quenched at rates a furnace cannot reach.

Numbers that follow from these specifications

  • A 0.05 µm length step on a 10 mm specimen is a strain of 5 × 10⁻⁶. The full γ → α step in section 1 is a few hundred times larger, so the limit on a start temperature is noise and thermal gradients, not resolution.
  • The solid quartz configuration stops at 1000 °C, so the planned 950 °C austenitisation for sample-01 fits with a solid Ø4 × 10 mm specimen. Anything above 1000 °C needs the alumina head and a hollow specimen.
  • Hollow specimens cool faster because there is less metal to cool and gas can flow through the bore. That buys rate, but less mass also means a larger relative effect of the thermocouple and of surface decarburisation.
  • Kop et al. used two type-S thermocouples on a Bähr 805A/D, one to control power and one to check homogeneity along the specimen. They agreed within 10 K. Quartz push rods expand at about 0.5 × 10⁻⁶ K⁻¹, against about 10 × 10⁻⁶ K⁻¹ for steel.
  • The measured change contains the expansion of the push rods and holder as well as the specimen. Alpha mode removes it with a calibration run: L₀ = Lm − Ls, measured minus system expansion.

Not every dilatometer works this way. The Adamel-Lhomargy DT 1000 used by García de Andrés's group heats Ø2 × 12 mm specimens with two tungsten-filament lamps in an elliptical reflector, measures through an amorphous silica push rod and LVDT to better than 0.1 µm, and quenches with a servo-controlled helium jet (Capdevila et al. 2003).

The rates in the table are what the controller can command. Near rapid cooling and at the end of a quench, the measured temperature lags or overshoots the programme. Yuxiang's note asks for a sacrificial specimen first, to measure the real rate through the ferrite range, overshoot and settling at the hold, the gradient along the 10 mm length and the acquisition rate.

3 · Where the length change comes from

FCC and BCC iron pack each atom into a different volume

Austenite is face-centred cubic with 4 iron atoms per cell, so each atom has a volume of aγ³/4. Ferrite is body-centred cubic with 2 atoms per cell, aα³/2. The difference is about one to two percent, and it depends on temperature and on the carbon dissolved in the austenite. For a specimen that changes volume equally in all directions, the length change is a third of the volume change: ΔL/L ≈ ΔV/3V.

4 · From length to fraction

The lever rule is exact only if the austenite does not change while it shrinks

The usual reading draws one tangent through the austenite line and one through the product line, and calls the transformed fraction the fraction of the way the curve has crossed from one to the other. That is correct when the product has one composition and the parent stays the same. Proeutectoid ferrite breaks the second condition. It rejects carbon into the remaining austenite, and the enriched austenite has a larger lattice than the tangent assumes.

flever(T) = [L(T) − Lγ(T)] / [Lα+P(T) − Lγ(T)]

What goes wrong, and in which direction

Carbon enrichment makes the remaining austenite bigger than the tangent predicts, so the specimen looks more expanded than its ferrite content alone would make it. The lever rule therefore reads too much ferrite during proeutectoid growth. The error disappears at the end, when no austenite is left, which is why the finish temperature is more reliable than the fraction curve in between. Section 1 shows the size of the error for the values you set.

How the analysis is corrected

The corrected methods compute the specimen length from the atomic volumes of each phase, with the austenite carbon updated by mass balance as ferrite forms, and solve for the fraction that reproduces the measured length. Pearlite is treated separately, as ferrite plus cementite formed from austenite of the composition it had when pearlite started. The correction is only as good as the lattice-parameter data, and it needs to know where pearlite starts, which comes from a change in slope or from metallography.

Kop et al.: fα = (V − Vγ)/(Vα − Vγ)  ·  fp = [V − Vγ + fα(Vγ − Vα)]/(Vp − Vγ)  ·  V = κV₀(3ΔL/L₀ + 1)
  • Vγ depends on the austenite carbon, which depends on fα, so the ferrite equation is solved iteratively. During pearlite fα is frozen and the austenite carbon is constant.
  • Pearlite is Vp = (1 − ρ)Vα + ρVθ, with ρ the cementite fraction. Cementite packs each iron atom into more volume than ferrite, so pearlite expands more than ferrite would. That is the second error in the lever rule.
  • κ scales the measured length to the calculated one, fitted before and after the transformation and varied linearly in between. It absorbs the push-rod contribution and transformation plasticity. Kop's κ were 1.004–1.008 on six C–Mn steels: the measured length change was always slightly smaller than calculated, and specimens came out shorter after a full cycle.
  • The switch from ferrite to pearlite is taken either where fα reaches its equilibrium value or at the second inflection point of L(T); the first inflection is the ferrite start. Kop preferred the inflection point, which is harder to see at high cooling rates.
  • Kop found the lever rule overestimates ferrite by up to 25 % (relative), with the largest error at the start of transformation and more error at higher carbon.

Three conditions sit under any fraction read from a dilatometer: the strain is isotropic, so the length is a third of the volume; the specimen has one temperature along its gauge length; and the products are known. Mn banding in a 1.9 wt% Mn steel can break the first, fast induction cycles the second, and overlapping ferrite, Widmanstätten, bainite and pearlite strains the third.

5 · Start temperatures

Where the curve leaves the line depends on how you look

A start temperature is where the curve first departs from the parent tangent. With real noise, "first" depends on the observer. Yang and Bhadeshia (2007) showed that judging the departure by eye gave Ms values with a standard deviation of 43 °C on one Fe–0.13C–2.3Mn–4Ni steel, measured on a DIL805 with Ø4 × 7 mm specimens cooled at 3.2–330 °C/s. The offset method fixes the criterion: draw a line parallel to the austenite tangent, offset by the strain that a chosen fraction of product would cause, and take the temperature where the curve crosses it. With a 1 vol% offset the scatter fell to 12 °C.

What the method buys and costs

  • The offset must sit well above the noise. Yang and Bhadeshia found an rms noise of 1.65 × 10⁻⁵ strain in the austenite fit, and their 1 vol% offset was roughly ten times larger.
  • A smaller offset reads a higher start temperature but triggers on noise. Their mean Ms rose from 373 °C at 1 % to 419 °C at 0.08 %.
  • An Ms quoted without its offset is incomplete. Quote the fraction it corresponds to.
  • The same idea applies to ferrite and to Ac1 on heating: define the start as a fixed small fraction, such as the 1 % and 99 % used for start and finish in ASM Vol. 4.
  • For Ms, Yang and Bhadeshia found that taking the derivative does not help. For ferrite followed by pearlite, Kop used inflection points of L(T) to find where pearlite takes over. Either way the derivative amplifies noise and needs smoothing.
  • The offset for 1 vol% comes from the lattice parameters of austenite and martensite (their Eqs. 2–4). For their steel it is 1.21 × 10⁻⁴. The paper's text says 1.21 × 10⁻³, which is a typo: the table and the equations both give 10⁻⁴. For sample-01 the same equations give 1.12 × 10⁻⁴ at 1 vol%, about 1.1 µm on a 10 mm specimen, against a 0.05 µm resolution.

The trace uses the austenite expansivity Yang and Bhadeshia measured (2.43 × 10⁻⁵ °C⁻¹), the full γ → α′ strain for sample-01 and a Koistinen–Marburger product curve. The 380 °C start is arbitrary. The noise default is the value Yang and Bhadeshia measured on a DIL805. Notice that noise alone moves the 1 % start by well under a degree. Their 12 °C scatter came from specimen to specimen, which they attributed to temperature non-uniformity and to the austenite grain structure.

6 · Heating

On heating the specimen shrinks, first as pearlite dissolves, then as ferrite does

Switch section 1 to heating. Austenite forms first from pearlite at Ac1, with a short steep contraction, then from proeutectoid ferrite up to Ac3. The measured Ac1 and Ac3 sit above the equilibrium Ae1 and Ae3 by an amount that grows with heating rate, which is why ASM Vol. 4 notes that continuous diagrams are delayed relative to isothermal ones, whether heating or cooling. A true isothermal diagram needs a very fast heating rate to the hold.

The start structure matters as much as the rate

In Xie Yi's report on two 0.25C–2.5Mn–1.4Si steels, tempering the quenched structure before heating raised Ac1 by 23–25 °C and Ac3 by 14–16 °C. The cause given is the carbide state and Cr and Mn partitioning. Those numbers belong to those steels and do not transfer to sample-01. The point is the method: measure Ac1 and Ac3 from the state you will actually start from.

Two things that distort a heating curve

  • Ferromagnetic ferrite heats more easily by induction, so the heating power drops at the Curie temperature. Kop used that drop as a check on the thermocouple, with TC = 1042 K − 1500 K × wMn. For sample-01 it gives about 740 °C, right next to Ae3 at 745 °C, so the power change overlaps the end of ferrite dissolution. Their steels had at most 0.72 wt% Mn, so this is an extrapolation.
  • Cementite in coarse pearlite may not dissolve by Ac3. Finishing above Ac3 does not guarantee homogeneous austenite. The 950 °C / 10 min austenitisation is about 200 K above Ae3 to make that likely.
7 · Isothermal holds

A hold turns the dilatometer into a clock

Quench from 950 °C to a hold temperature fast enough that nothing forms on the way, then record length against time. At constant temperature the parent and product tangents are flat, so the length change is directly proportional to the transformed fraction, apart from the carbon-enrichment term. Ferrite at 640 °C in sample-01 stops short of full transformation. The plateau is set by how much carbon the austenite can take, and with 1.92 wt% Mn that limit is between the paraequilibrium and the full-equilibrium values. That is the question of the PLE, NPLE and paraequilibrium lesson.

Reading a hold

  • The JMAK form f = fmax[1 − exp(−k tⁿ)] is a fitting curve here. The exponent and time scale are sliders, not values for sample-01.
  • The interruptions at 10 s, 30 s, 2 min and 10 min are the ones planned for sample-01. Each is quenched, so the remaining austenite becomes martensite and the specimen records the ferrite at that moment for metallography or EBSD.
  • A dilatometer sees how much product formed, not what shape it has. Allotriomorphic ferrite, primary and secondary Widmanstätten plates and bainitic ferrite all give the same kind of expansion. Separating them needs the interrupted specimens, which is where your in-situ confocal and EBSD work comes in. See Planar to Widmanstätten Ferrite.
  • Capdevila, Caballero and García de Andrés (2003) measured allotriomorphic and idiomorphic ferrite separately in a 0.37C–1.45Mn steel by point counting, and concluded that treating proeutectoid ferrite as one reaction in a dilatometric analysis "might lead to wrong conclusions".
  • A change in rate partway through a hold, visible as a kink in log time, can mark a new product taking over. Use it to choose interruption times rather than as evidence of a morphology change on its own.
8 · Under load

Deformation and tension modules let you transform steel under stress

The DIL805A/D can compress a specimen at up to 20 kN or pull it at up to 10 kN, before or during a thermal cycle, while still recording length. That opens two kinds of experiment. You can deform the austenite first and then watch how it transforms, which is how hot rolling is simulated. Or you can hold a stress while the transformation happens, and see how the stress changes the start temperature and the strain.

Three effects of stress

  • Ms moves. Tension raises Ms by about 0.05 K/MPa in 60NCD11, and hydrostatic pressure lowers it by about the same (−0.05 K/MPa measured on Fe–0.5C). Compression also raises Ms, by less, because the transformation shear dominates the dilatation (γ₀ ≈ 0.19 against ε₀ ≈ 0.03). A shear stress that suits a variant does mechanical work towards the transformation, so less chemical driving force is needed.
  • Transformation plasticity. A permanent strain builds up while the transformation runs, under a stress below the yield stress of either phase. It is linear in stress up to the austenite yield stress, so the axial strain overshoots the stress-free curve while the diameter shrinks. Two mechanisms are proposed. Greenwood–Johnson: the weaker phase yields locally to accommodate the volume change, biased by the stress, giving εpt = (5/6)(σ/Y)(ΔV/V). Magee: the stress selects favourable variants, so their shape strains add up instead of cancelling. Denis et al. found 60NCD11 closer to Greenwood–Johnson.
  • Diffusional reactions start sooner or later. Uniaxial tension or compression shortens the incubation of ferrite and pearlite, and hydrostatic pressure prolongs it and lowers A1 and A3.
  • Removing the stress can stop the reaction. In interrupted tensile tests, martensite stopped forming when the load came off, and further cooling was needed to restart it.

With a stress applied, length is no longer a third of volume. The axial and diametral strains split apart, so a fraction from length alone is wrong. Measuring the diameter as well, using the optical channel shown in the DIL805 deck, lets you recover volume as εV ≈ εaxial + 2εdiam.

Δgmech = σn·δ + τ·s   →   uniaxial σa: Δgmech = σa(δ sin²χ₀ + s sin χ₀ cos λ₀)

Patel and Cohen's work term, as written by Christian (§91); Denis et al. give the matching dMs/dσ = ½[γ₀ sin 2θ ± ε₀(1 + cos 2θ)]/(dΔG/dT), + for tension. σn and τ are the stress components normal to and along the habit plane; δ and s are the dilatation and shear of the invariant-plane strain; χ₀ and λ₀ are the angles from the specimen axis to the habit plane and to the shape-strain direction. The variant with the largest Δgmech forms first, like Schmid's law for slip. Ms rises roughly linearly with stress because the chemical driving force is roughly linear in temperature. Uniaxial compression also raises Ms, by less than tension, because the dilatation δ then works against it.

Stress-assisted martensite

Above Ms, an elastic stress supplies the missing driving force and martensite nucleates on the existing sites. The start temperature rises with stress.

Strain-induced martensite

Above a certain temperature, the stress needed exceeds the austenite yield stress. Plastic strain then creates new nucleation sites, such as shear-band intersections, and martensite forms up to a limit called Md. Christian notes that one favoured variant then dominates, instead of the self-accommodating groups seen without stress.

Deform, then transform

Compress the austenite at a set temperature and strain rate, then cool. Dislocations and deformation bands add ferrite nucleation sites. Compare the transformation start with an undeformed specimen to measure the effect.

Minimum load matters

Even in the quenching module the push rods press on the specimen. That small load can cause transformation plasticity on its own. Use the minimum contact load the instrument allows, and keep it the same between runs.

Yuxiang's note keeps deformation out of the first campaign until the undeformed baseline is established. It is the natural second step if prior-austenite deformation turns out to change the Widmanstätten start.

What to check on the dilatometer

  • Run a full cooling curve first, at the cooling rate you will use to reach 640 °C. If the trace leaves the austenite tangent before 640 °C, ferrite formed on the way and the hold is not isothermal from the start.
  • Measure Ac1 and Ac3 on the heating ramp of every run. They confirm the start structure and show whether 950 °C is safely austenitic.
  • Record the overshoot and settling time at 640 °C. A 10 s interruption is meaningless if the hold takes several seconds to settle.
  • On each final quench, read the martensite expansion. Its size gives the austenite left at the interruption, an independent check on the ferrite fraction.
  • Measure Ms with the offset method on the final quench. The two handbook formulas disagree by 95 K for this steel, so the dilatometer is the only reliable number.
  • Keep the full time, temperature and length data, not only the extracted temperatures.
