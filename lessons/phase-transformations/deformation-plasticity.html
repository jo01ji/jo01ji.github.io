Phase transformations · ferrite morphology

Deformation and plasticity in phase selection

A ferrite plate changes the shape of the austenite it replaces as well as its composition. This page follows that shape change from the nucleus to the growing tip: how it raises the nucleation barrier, how interface defects produce it, how its elastic anisotropy picks the plate shape, and how plastic relaxation at the tip slows the plate down without changing what kind of plate it is.

1 · Nucleation

Strain energy is subtracted from the driving force, and the barrier goes as its inverse square

The pillbox model used for grain-boundary and sympathetic nucleation of ferrite writes the critical-nucleus energy with a denominator Φ, the sum of the volume free-energy change and the elastic strain energy. The volume term drives nucleation and the strain term holds it back, so strain energy shrinks |Φ|, and the barrier rises as 1/Φ².

“Φ is the sum of the Helmholtz volume free energy change ΔGv driving nucleation and the elastic strain energy usually acting to restrain nucleation.”[A1995] text under Eq. 3, p. 115

Φ is Aaronson's symbol, the same one behind ΦSN/ΦFull in the sympathetic nucleation lesson. The ratio uses only the 1/Φ² form of Eq. 3 (σ, ε and the site geometry cancel). The rate ratio uses J ∝ exp(−ΔG*/kT) from Eq. 2 of the same paper and needs a strain-free barrier, which is an input here, not a value from any steel.

Why the square matters

A strain term worth one fifth of the chemical driving force raises the barrier by about 56 %. Because the rate depends on the barrier exponentially, that is several orders of magnitude in J. This is why the 1995 overview says the critical nucleus shape is “by far the most important factor governing nucleation kinetics” at small driving forces, and why it treats its strain-free pillbox analysis as a simplification: the analysis is done “in the absence of the considerable complications introduced by dilatometric and shear strain energies.” [A1995, p. 114]

A pillbox is bounded entirely by low-energy coherent faces, which the overview calls “some highly qualitative justification” for the model. [A1995, p. 114]

Strain energy enters twice in what follows: as a penalty in Φ at nucleation, and as a stored energy in the grown plate (§7). Bhadeshia's 50 J mol⁻¹ for Widmanstätten ferrite, the number behind your sample-01 Ws estimate, is the second kind.

2 · The shape change

An invariant-plane strain, and why two opposing plates cost far less than one

An invariant-plane strain (IPS) leaves one plane, the habit plane, unrotated and undistorted. Every point moves parallel to one direction by an amount proportional to its distance from that plane. A plate that crosses a polished surface therefore tilts the surface over its width, which is the surface relief seen on Widmanstätten ferrite, bainite and martensite. Bhadeshia uses the form of that relief to explain why Widmanstätten ferrite stores so much less strain energy than bainite.

“The displacive formation of Widmanstätten ferrite seems to involve the cooperative growth of mutually accommodating crystallographic variants. The resulting surface relief thus consists of two adjacent and opposing invariant-plane strains, so that the elastically accommodated strain energy accompanying plate formation is rather small (of the order of 50 J mol⁻¹). Bainite plates … show surface-relief effects corresponding to single invariant-plane strains, so that the strain-energy term is larger, amounting to about 400 J mol⁻¹.”[B1985] p. 501

Reading the picture

The habit plane here is vertical and the shape change is drawn unconstrained, as if the plate were free to move. A single plate shears everything to its right by s·t, where t is the plate thickness. A real matrix cannot do that, so the misfit is stored as long-range elastic energy or relaxed by plastic flow.

With two adjacent plates of opposite shear, the surface forms a tent and the vertical offset beyond the pair returns to zero. The shear no longer reaches the far matrix. The dilatation does not cancel, because both plates expand, so the pair still carries some misfit. This is what Bhadeshia's “two adjacent and opposing invariant-plane strains” means, and why the pair costs about 50 J mol⁻¹ against about 400 J mol⁻¹ for a single bainite plate.

The strain values on the sliders are for drawing only. Neither source gives the IPS shear or dilatation of Widmanstätten ferrite in your steel.

Martensite has the same geometry with no diffusion at all. The full crystallography (Bain strain, lattice-invariant shear, habit plane) is in the martensite traces lesson, and variant pairing in the KS variant atlas.

3 · Where the shape strain comes from

A train of disconnections moves the interface and shears what it leaves behind

Howe, Pond and Hirth describe a transformation interface as terraces separated by disconnections: line defects that are both a step of height h and a dislocation with Burgers vector b. When one sweeps along the interface, the interface advances by h and the material it converts is strained by b. A train of them therefore produces a plastic shape change whose components are b/h, on the terrace plane. That is an invariant-plane strain made by defect motion.

“When h is finite, the defect is a disconnection, characterised by (b, h) … The long-range elastic field is that of the dislocation, while the overlap step produces no long-range elastic field but does produce an offset of the interface.”[H2009] §2.1, p. 795

What the animation shows

Each pass of a step converts one layer of γ, of thickness h, to α. The fiducial lines stay straight in the γ below and kink where they enter the new α, because each new layer is sheared by bx. The old α above the original interface is not strained; it is translated, which shows at a free surface as relief. Eq. 17 of the paper gives the strain of a train with spacing dD moving by dy as (dy/dD)·b·n, and the volume converted as h·dy/dD. Their ratio is b/h. [H2009, §3.2, pp. 805–806]

The paper separates three kinetic classes. Fast martensite: disconnections move at close to the speed of sound. Slow martensite: kink pairs. Diffusional transformations: also kink-pair nucleation and propagation, but “the kink motion is diffusion controlled as the components have to interchange to produce the composition change.” [H2009, §3.1, p. 805]

This is the bridge between the diffusional and the displacive views of Widmanstätten ferrite. The same defect carries the shape change and, through its kinks, the diffusion. Howe et al. also note that an exact invariant plane is “a sufficient but not necessary condition” for a glissile semi-coherent interface. [H2009, p. 805]

4 · Cottura, Appolaire, Finel & Le Bouar, Acta Mater. 72 (2014)

The diffusion problem allows any tip radius; elastic anisotropy picks one

A parabolic tip growing at constant speed into a supersaturated matrix satisfies the diffusion equation for a whole family of radius and velocity pairs. Ivantsov's solution fixes only their product, through the Péclet number P = RpVp/2D. Something else has to choose the point on the curve. In solidification it is the anisotropy of the interface energy. Cottura et al. note that phase-field models of Widmanstätten ferrite needed interface anisotropies “with orders of magnitude which may legitimately be questioned” [C2014, p. 201], and they show that an anisotropic transformation eigenstrain does the job instead.

5 · Cottura, Appolaire, Finel & Le Bouar, Scripta Mater. 108 (2015)

Plastic relaxation at the tip: a blunter, slower plate of the same kind

The usual argument for leaving elasticity out of diffusional growth models is that the austenite is soft at the reaction temperature and simply yields. Cottura et al. tested this by adding strain-gradient viscoplasticity to their 2014 model, with only the matrix allowed to flow. The plate still grows at a constant rate. Plastic flow, confined mainly to the region just ahead of the tip, relaxes the coherency stresses there, and the tip settles at a larger radius and a lower velocity.

Their four conclusions

  1. The case without mechanics is not a limit of plastic relaxation: “with a shear dominated eigenstrain, neglecting elasticity would simply generate circular precipitates.”
  2. Stationary growth survives isotropic plasticity, with or without a plastic length: “plasticity only modifies the couple tip radius/growth rate.”
  3. Conventional viscoplasticity is the upper bound for relaxation and so the lower bound for the lengthening rate. Its effect resembles a smaller eigenstrain, but probably not quantitatively, because the plastic strain is so inhomogeneous.
  4. The plastic length scale has only a moderate effect and “cannot be considered as the main selection mechanism for the tip radius.”

[C2015, p. 120]

Why ξ = 10 nm relaxes less

The intrinsic plastic length ξ = √(A/E) (ξ̃ in the paper) comes from a gradient term in the plastic free energy. When plasticity is confined to a small region in front of the tip, that gradient term hardens it. So with ξ = 10 nm the plastic strain varies more smoothly and is weaker, and the plate behaves closer to the elastic case; increasing ξ pushes it further that way. [C2015, Eq. 6–8, p. 118; Fig. 3 discussion, p. 120]

The same plastic flow is the origin of the stored energy in the grown plate. Bhattacharya et al.: “At higher temperature, lower yield-strength of γ causes the plastic relaxation which results in large dislocation-density. The precipitated α′ inherits these dislocations which ultimately raises its stored energy.” [Bh2017, §2.3.1, p. 320]

Their parameters are generic, “not those of a particular alloy” [p. 118], and they warn the model “may not be directly applied to study the growth of acicular structures in steels, in particular sheaves of subunits as more commonly observed in bainite.” [p. 120]

6 · Denis, Gautier, Simon & Beck, Mater. Sci. Technol. 1 (1985)

Under an applied stress the shape change becomes a measurable strain

Denis et al. separate two effects of stress on a transformation in steel. The “metallurgical interaction” changes the kinetics and “sometimes leads to a different morphology in the phase produced”. Transformation plasticity is a permanent strain that appears during transformation even when the applied stress is below the yield stress. [D1985, p. 805] Two mechanisms are offered for the second.

Two mechanisms

Greenwood–Johnson. The volume change sets up internal stresses; an applied stress, added to them, makes the weaker phase yield in its own direction below the macroscopic yield stress. Eq. 3, built on the Levy–von Mises criterion. For the 60NCD11 steel the measured values fell between the two relations, much closer to Greenwood–Johnson. [D1985, p. 808, Fig. 5 p. 807]

Magee. Under stress, the product chooses the orientations that minimise the total energy including the work of the applied stress, so plates whose shape strain helps the stress form preferentially. This version works even when ΔV/V is very small, and is reversible in thermoelastic alloys. [D1985, p. 808]

For martensite they conclude both operate and “the contribution of each mechanism is however difficult to predict quantitatively.” [p. 808]

Magee's mechanism is the IPS of §2 seen from outside: an applied stress does work on the shape strain, so it selects variants. In your DIL805A/D runs under load, this is what the stress section of the dilatometry lesson is about.

Your own sensitivity check gives about 682 °C at 30 J mol⁻¹ and 660 °C at 70 J mol⁻¹, so the mechanical term moves Ws by roughly ±10 K for ±20 J mol⁻¹.
  • That 50 J mol⁻¹ is a relaxation product. On Bhattacharya's reading (§5), it is the dislocation density the plate inherits from plastic relaxation of the austenite. Cottura's results say the same relaxation changes the tip radius and speed. So the stored energy and the lengthening rate are tied to one process, which none of the Ws criteria treat explicitly.
  • Elastic selection as a candidate mechanism. The planar-to-Widmanstätten lesson lists explanations A–D for the morphological transition. Cottura's result (§4) is a selection mechanism of the D kind that does not depend on interface-energy anisotropy.

9 · Open questions

What this page leaves unresolved

  • Cottura's models are 2-D with 3-D elasticity, one generic eigenstrain, isotropic plasticity and no carbon–Mn chemistry. No paper in your folder runs the equivalent calculation for a hypoeutectoid Fe–C–Mn steel with a measured Widmanstätten eigenstrain. They list anisotropic viscoplasticity (slip systems) and colonies of plates as next steps. [C2015, p. 120]
  • The disconnection description (§3) and the elastic-anisotropy description (§4) are of the same interface at different scales. Neither paper connects disconnection density and Burgers vector to the tip-selection result.
  • Bhadeshia's 50 and 400 J mol⁻¹ are single values. How the stored energy depends on temperature, through the austenite yield stress that sets the plastic relaxation, is not addressed in these sources.
  • Denis et al. (1985) leave the balance between Greenwood–Johnson and Magee unresolved, and this folder holds nothing more recent on transformation plasticity.
