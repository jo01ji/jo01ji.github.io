Metallographic etching: steels and stainless steels

A polished section looks almost featureless until it is etched. What follows is why that is, what is actually happening at the surface when an etchant works, and a working reference for the reagents used on carbon, low-alloy and stainless steels — what each one is made of, which constituent it attacks, and which it leaves alone.

1 · A short history

Etching is four centuries older than the microstructure it reveals

Etching with acid to reveal pattern in metal is old. What is young, barely more than 160 years, is the idea of etching a polished surface and looking at it down a microscope to see the grains and phases themselves. The gap between those two is the story of how metallography began.

Dates and attributions from ASM Handbook Vol. 9, “Metallography: An Introduction” (p. 3–20) and C.S. Smith, A History of Metallography (1960).

Before the microscope

Macroetching to bring out the watered pattern of Damascus sword blades and armour predates microscopy “by at least four centuries” (ASM Vol. 9, p. 4). In 1808, Alois von Widmanstätten and Carl von Schreibers in Vienna etched iron meteorites in nitric acid to reveal the coarse crystalline pattern now named for Widmanstätten — then inked the etched surface and printed it onto paper, a nature print. These were structures for the naked eye.

Réaumur and the study of fracture

A century earlier, R.A.F. de Réaumur (1720s) had studied steel through its fracture surfaces and quenching, “that prince of applied scientists” in Smith’s phrase — the first systematic attempt to connect how steel broke to what it was made of, but still without a prepared, etched microsection.

“Widmanstätten and Schreibers etched specimens that could be viewed with the naked eye, but Sorby was the first to etch specimens and observe the true microstructure with a microscope.”ASM Handbook Vol. 9 (2004), “Metallography: An Introduction,” p. 4

Henry Clifton Sorby, 1863

Sorby (1826–1908) was a Sheffield geologist who had developed thin-section methods for rocks. In 1863, by way of an interest in the crystalline structure of meteorites, he turned the same care on artificial steel: he cut and polished a specimen to remove “all traces of roughness,” then etched it in extremely dilute nitric acid, watching the etch so as not to overdo it. He insisted the final polish “must not be one which gives bright reflection but one which may show all the irregularities of the material.” From this he identified ferrite, graphite, cementite, pearlite and the phosphide eutectic, recognised that iron is made of crystal grains, and inferred that iron changes allotropically on heating — all essentially correct, and from scratch.

Why it took until 1863

Metals are opaque, so unlike biological samples they need reflected-light microscopy and therefore a carefully prepared flat surface — which is why the metallurgical microscope arrives two centuries after the biological one. Smith notes Sorby’s work was ignored for twenty years, revived in the 1880s only when industrial pressure and Osmond’s link to Troost and Le Chatelier joined structural observation to thermal analysis. Very fine unresolved pearlite was for a time called “sorbite” in his honour; the name he does keep is “pearlite,” from the “mother of pearl” look he described.

The last piece is a cautionary one, and it is why your training matters. In the 1930s José Ramón Vilella showed that a layer of “distorted or disturbed” metal left by coarse grinding produces false structures — what early workers mistook for “sorbite” or “troostite-sorbite” was often just damaged pearlite. His fix, alternate etching and polishing to strip that layer, is the ancestor of the etch-and-repolish cycles still used today (ASM Vol. 9, p. 5). Good etching starts with honest polishing.

2 · Why etch at all

A mirror-polished surface reflects light uniformly, which hides almost everything

Only features with a large enough difference in reflectivity, or enough relief, show up unetched — cracks, pores, pits, and nonmetallic inclusions. The eye cannot resolve the small reflectivity differences between grains or phases on a bare polished surface, so contrast has to be manufactured.

“Although some information may be obtained from as-polished specimens, the microstructure is usually visible only after etching. Only features that exhibit a 10% or greater difference in reflectivity can be viewed without etching.”ASM Handbook Vol. 9 (2004), “Contrast Enhancement and Etching,” p. 294
Etching

Preferential chemical, electrochemical, or physical attack of different constituents on a polished surface, to reveal structure for macroscopic or microscopic examination.

Microetching

Reveals the microstructure for observation at 50× or above — the everyday use of the word.

Macroetching

Reveals coring, seams, forging bursts and flow lines to the unaided eye, at 50× or less.

Tint (colour) etching

Deposits a thin interference film selectively on one constituent instead of dissolving the surface, so contrast comes from film thickness rather than relief.

This page follows the two ASM Handbook Vol. 9 (2004) articles that cover the ground: “Contrast Enhancement and Etching” (p. 294–312) for mechanism and nomenclature, Bramfitt & Lawrence’s “Metallography and Microstructures of Carbon and Low-Alloy Steels” (p. 608–626) and the low-carbon-sheet-steel article (p. 588–607) for the ferritic/pearlitic reagents, and Vander Voort, Lucas & Manilova’s “Metallography and Microstructures of Stainless Steels and Maraging Steels” (p. 670–700) for the stainless reagents.

3 · The chemistry

Most etching is corrosion, deliberately made uneven

Chemical and electrolytic etching is controlled corrosion: a cathodic (reduction) reaction absorbs electrons somewhere on the surface while an anodic (oxidation) reaction releases them, with metal leaving the surface as ions at the anodic sites.

“The chemical methods of etching processes involve a process of controlled corrosion from cathodic (reducing) reactions and anodic (oxidizing) reactions… Acids attack all elements preceding hydrogen (H2) as it evolves… Therefore, microstructural elements of different electrochemical potential are attacked at varying rates, producing differential etching, which results in microstructural contrast.”ASM Handbook Vol. 9 (2004), “Contrast Enhancement and Etching,” p. 296

The anodic half of a nital etch, written simply

Fe(s) → Fe²⁺(aq) + 2e⁻ (anodic: metal dissolves) 2H⁺(aq) + 2e⁻ → H₂(g) (cathodic: acid is reduced)

This is the generic iron/acid redox couple taught in any corrosion text, not a reaction specific to nital — with concentrated HNO₃ the reduction product is usually NO or NO₂ rather than H₂, and the handbook’s own worked equations are given as a figure rather than text, so treat this as the textbook skeleton, not a quoted formula.

Why some spots dissolve faster than others

  • Grain boundaries have more structural disorder and segregated impurities, so they sit at a different potential than the grain interior — this is why boundaries etch as dark lines before the grains inside them darken.
  • In a two-phase alloy, one phase is anodic relative to the other. The anodic phase is attacked; the cathodic phase is barely touched. The potential gap between two different phases is larger than the gap between a grain boundary and its interior, which is why multiphase steels etch faster and more selectively than single-phase ones.
  • With ordinary chemical etchants, the same phase is almost always anodic in a given reagent — you cannot usually flip which phase gets attacked just by etching longer. Reversing which phase is anodic needs a different reagent, or potentiostatic control of the applied voltage.

This is the logic behind having dozens of named reagents instead of one. Each reagent sets up a different set of anodic and cathodic sites on the same surface, so each one reads out a different feature. The next section writes out the actual reactions where the sources document them.

4 · The reactions, written out

What is actually forming at the surface

For most everyday acid etchants the published sources give the mechanism (anodic dissolution balanced by a cathodic reduction) but not a single tidy balanced equation, because the real products depend on acid, concentration and what reduces at the cathodic sites. Where a reagent’s chemistry is written down, it is usually a film-forming or staining reagent. Here is what each source actually states, and where it stops.

1 · the general couple Anodic dissolution, cathodic reduction
anodic: Fe → Fe²⁺ + 2e⁻ cathodic: 2H⁺ + 2e⁻ → H₂↑ (non-noble metal, non-oxidizing acid) or: reduction of dissolved O₂ / the oxidizer

Vander Voort: the anodic metal atoms are first adsorbed, then hydrated by water dipoles and carried off as cations; “complexly bound ions or relatively insoluble metal compounds may be formed and precipitated onto the metal surface” instead (p. 168–169). In etching a non-noble metal in a non-oxidizing acid, the liberated hydrogen is the electron acceptor; noble metals need an oxidizer (nitric, chromic, picric acid, ferric chloride, persulfate, peroxide) to release oxygen as the acceptor (p. 169).

2 · nital Nitric acid in alcohol — mechanism, not a quoted equation

The handbook describes nital as an attack etchant: “The nitric acid in this etchant chemically attacks the ferrite grains” (Bramfitt & Lawrence, p. 612). Neither ASM Vol. 9 nor Vander Voort prints a balanced Fe + HNO₃ equation, because with concentrated nitric the reduction product is typically NO or NO₂ rather than H₂, and the net reaction is not single-valued. Treat the couple above as the skeleton; do not quote a specific balanced nital equation as if the handbook gave one.

3 · Murakami’s reagent Alkaline ferricyanide — a documented reaction
2K₃Fe(CN)₆ + 2KOH → 2K₄Fe(CN)₆ + H₂O + ½O₂

Vander Voort gives this (after Kegley), with the key point that “the ferricyanide acts as an oxidizer, however, whether or not a particular constituent is etched depends on the passivation response of the constituent by either nascent oxygen or excess alkali” (p. 230). The nascent oxygen is what attacks carbides and σ phase while the ferrite/austenite matrix passivates.

4 · metabisulfite tint Decomposition to a sulfide staining film
Na₂S₂O₅ + H₂O (on the metal surface) → SO₂ + H₂S + … → sulfide film

Vander Voort: in aqueous solution the metabisulfite salt “decomposes on contact with the metal surface, producing SO₂, H₂S, and H₂”; on stainless steel the SO₂ depassivates the surface and the H₂S supplies sulfur ions that combine with Fe or Ni ions to grow the staining film (p. 175). The film is thickest on the anodic constituents, so ferrite and austenite colour while carbides and nitrides stay bright.

5 · tint film colour Interference, not dissolution

A tint etchant deposits a transparent film tens to hundreds of nanometres thick. The colour you see is thin-film interference: it is set by the film thickness, which in turn depends on each constituent’s reactivity and crystallographic orientation. This is why the colours from a Beraha or Klemm reagent are only loosely reproducible while the response (what films and what stays bright) is reliable (Vander Voort p. 173–175; ASM Vol. 9 p. 298–300).

6 · electrolytic The applied current replaces the oxidizer

In electrolytic (anodic) etching the external current does the oxidizing: metal ions leave the specimen (the anode) while electrons flow out through the circuit. If those ions meet non-metal ions from the electrolyte and form an insoluble compound, a precipitated film grows instead of clean dissolution (ASM Vol. 9 p. 305) — the basis of the voltage-selective etches further down.

The honest summary for a trainee: you can always name the roles (what is anodic, what is cathodic, what the oxidizer is) and for the alkaline and tint reagents you can write the reaction down. For a plain acid attack etch, resist the urge to hand someone a neat Fe + HNO₃ equation — the sources deliberately don’t, because it isn’t single-valued.

5 · Rules of thumb

Why acids outline carbides, and what it takes to attack the carbide itself

Vander Voort notes that most etchants were found by trial and error, and that the job of each ingredient “cannot always be determined with certainty” (p. 169). A handful of patterns still explain most of what you see on steels.

Rule 1 · the recipe A corrosive agent, a modifier, and an oxidizer

“Chemical etchants usually have three main components: a corrosive agent (such as hydrochloric, sulfuric, phosphoric, or acetic acid), a modifier (such as alcohol or glycerin) that reduces ionization, and an oxidizer (such as hydrogen peroxide, Fe³⁺, or Cu²⁺). In some etchants one component serves two of these functions, such as nitric acid in nital. In electrolytic etchants, the applied current acts as the oxidizer.” (p. 166)

Read any recipe this way. Marshall’s reagent is H₂SO₄ + oxalic acid (corrosive) with H₂O₂ (oxidizer). Kalling’s is HCl (corrosive) with ethanol (modifier) and CuCl₂ (Cu²⁺ oxidizer). Glyceregia is HCl with HNO₃ (oxidizer) moderated by glycerol.

Rule 2 · acid etchants Carbides are cathodic, so acids dissolve the ferrite around them

“The cementite lamellae are cathodic to the ferrite lamellae, with a substantial potential difference. Etching with 4% picric acid in alcohol (picral) produces a slow, uniform, smooth dissolution of the ferrite lamellae, with the cathodic cementite lamellae in relief.” (p. 167) Under high magnification “the cementite lamellae are not attacked but appear outlined because of dissolution of the ferrite lamellae” (p. 168).

So nital, picral and Vilella’s do not etch cementite. They show it by recessing and roughening the ferrite next to it. The same applies to σ phase and alloy carbides in stainless steel under most acid reagents.

Rule 3 · nital vs picral Nital is sensitive to orientation; picral is not

Picral gives uniform darkening of pearlite colonies. Nital “produces a wide range of gray-level contrast because of the orientation sensitivity of nital”, and some lightly etched pearlite colonies “may be mistaken for ferrite” (p. 167–168).

The same orientation sensitivity is what lets nital show ferrite grains and their boundaries, which picral does not. Use picral to measure pearlite fraction and nital to measure ferrite grain size.

Rule 4 · alkaline oxidizing reagents To attack the carbide itself, switch to a hot alkaline oxidizer

Boiling alkaline sodium picrate colours cementite only (Fig. 3-37, p. 213). Murakami’s (alkaline ferricyanide) and Groesbeck’s (alkaline permanganate) stain carbides and σ while the ferrite and austenite matrix stays bright. For Murakami’s, Vander Voort gives the reaction and the reason (p. 230):

2K₃Fe(CN)₆ + 2KOH → 2K₄Fe(CN)₆ + H₂O + ½O₂

“The ferricyanide acts as an oxidizer, however, whether or not a particular constituent is etched depends on the passivation response of the constituent by either nascent oxygen or excess alkali.” (p. 230)

Rule 5 · stainless steels Chloride breaks the passive film, and the oxidizer is kept mild

Stainless steel passivates, so its etchants rely on reducing acids (HCl, H₂SO₄, HF), with alcohol or glycerol lowering dissociation so that strong acid stays controllable (ASM Vol. 9, p. 679). The oxidizer is often Cu²⁺ (Kalling’s, Fry’s, Marble’s) rather than nitric acid on its own, which tends to keep the film intact.

Rule 6 · tint etchants Films grow on anodic phases or cathodic phases, never both

Beraha classified tint etchants as anodic reagents, which film and colour only the anodic constituents, cathodic reagents, which film only the cathodic ones, and complex reagents (p. 175). Metabisulfite decomposes on the surface to SO₂ and H₂S. SO₂ depassivates stainless steel and H₂S supplies sulfide for the film, so “the anodic microconstituents, i.e., ferrite and austenite, are colored, while the cathodic constituents, i.e., carbides and nitrides, are bright and unaffected” (p. 175). Selenic-acid and molybdate reagents do the opposite: they are reduced and deposit on the cathodic areas.

Pearlite in section after a picral etch

Schematic, not to scale. The ferrite between the cementite plates is dissolved back below the polish plane and roughened, so it scatters light and looks dark in bright field. The cementite is untouched and stays at the original plane. When the lamellae are finer than the microscope can resolve, the whole colony just reads as dark.

My reading, not stated in either source: one likely reason the selectivity flips in alkali is that iron-rich ferrite and austenite passivate in strong alkali, while chromium-rich carbides and σ can be oxidized to soluble chromate. Kegley’s passivation statement above supports this, but neither source gives the chromate step, so treat it as a hypothesis.

Overetching can reverse the picture. If etching continues too long, “the phase that was initially anodic may undergo a change in potential, perhaps by deposition of reaction products… If this occurs, the phase that was originally cathodic can undergo dissolution”. Vander Voort’s rule of thumb is that “as the magnification increases, the degree of etch attack must be reduced” (p. 167).

6 · Carbon and low-alloy steels

Nital draws ferrite boundaries; picral draws carbide boundaries

“The nitric acid in this etchant chemically attacks the ferrite grains… The boundaries between the grains can be seen, because the reflected light… is scattered.”Bramfitt & Lawrence, ASM Handbook Vol. 9, p. 612
“[Picral] highlights the boundaries around carbides… Note… the picral etch did not delineate the ferrite grain boundaries.”Bramfitt & Lawrence, ASM Handbook Vol. 9, p. 612

In a very-low-carbon interstitial-free steel the handbook found 2% nital revealed only a few ferrite boundaries while Marshall’s reagent revealed essentially all of them — a practical reason Marshall’s is preferred for ferrite grain-size measurement in clean, low-carbon steel.

7 · A thesis-relevant case

Revealing prior-austenite grain boundaries in a steel that is no longer austenite

For allotriomorphic ferrite and Widmanstätten sideplates, the prior-austenite grain boundary is the nucleation site and the reference frame for everything that grows from it — so being able to see it in a quenched or partially transformed sample matters as much as seeing the ferrite itself.

Vilella’s reagent

1 g picric acid 5 mL hydrochloric acid (conc.) 95 mL ethyl alcohol

“Good for ferrite-carbide structures. Produces grain contrast for estimating prior-austenite grain size. Results best on martensite tempered at 300–500 °C (570–930 °F). Occasionally reveals prior-austenite grain boundaries in high-alloy steels.”
— Bramfitt & Lawrence, ASM Handbook Vol. 9, p. 615

Winsteard’s reagent (modified)

Part A: 2 g picric acid, 10 mL ethyl alcohol Part B: 200 mL water, 5 mL sodium dodecylbenzene sulfonate (40% soln.), 5 drops HCl

“To delineate prior austenite grain boundaries.” — same source, p. 615. The picric acid in alcohol (A) is mixed with the aqueous surfactant (B) just before use.

These reagents depend on impurity segregation to the old boundary. Vander Voort: “Some phosphorus segregates to grain boundaries during austenitization, and additional segregation occurs during aging between about 350 and 575 °C. The presence of phosphorus at the austenite grain boundaries has been shown to promote grain-boundary etching with saturated aqueous picric acid”, while steels free of phosphorus do not etch this way (p. 224). Grange’s workaround for difficult samples was a deliberate embrittling hold, 16 h at 510 °C, before etching in boiling saturated aqueous picric acid. That revealed the austenite boundaries even in ferrite–pearlite (p. 224). For your own samples, a sample with very low P may not show prior-austenite boundaries with picric acid at all, and an extra temper-embrittling hold is the classic remedy. See Reading a dilatometer for where these boundaries sit in a DIL805 trace, and Planar to Widmanstätten ferrite for why the boundary's crystallography, not just its position, controls what nucleates there.

8 · Stainless steels

The thing that makes stainless steel corrosion-resistant also makes it hard to etch

A chromium oxide passive film is exactly what most etchants rely on an active (non-passivating) acid to prevent. Reducing acids — HCl, H₂SO₄, HF — are preferred over oxidizing ones; nitric acid is used only in combination, as aqua regia or with a chloride, because on its own it tends to repassivate the surface rather than attack it.

“As the corrosion resistance of the alloys increases, stronger and stronger etchants must be used… Because alcohol reduces dissociation, alcohol-based reagents can be made more concentrated without becoming too powerful for controlled etching. Stainless steel surfaces passivate; therefore, reducing conditions are preferred to oxidizing conditions that promote passivity.”Vander Voort, Lucas & Manilova, ASM Handbook Vol. 9, p. 679

Etching gets harder, in roughly this order: ferritic & martensitic grades → precipitation-hardenable & maraging grades → austenitic grades → duplex grades, where ferrite and austenite each need a different reagent to see clearly in the same field of view.

9 · Electrolytic etching

Applying a voltage turns "etch for longer" into "etch at a chosen rate"

The specimen is the anode; a counter-electrode (often platinum or stainless steel) is the cathode; both sit in the etchant, now acting as electrolyte, under an external DC supply.

“During electrolytic etching, positive metal ions leave the specimen surface and diffuse into the electrolyte; an equivalent number of electrons remain in the material… However, if the metal ions leaving the material react with nonmetal ions from the electrolyte and form an insoluble compound, precipitated layers will form on the specimen surface.”

— ASM Handbook Vol. 9 (2004), “Contrast Enhancement and Etching,” p. 305

This is why electrolytic etchants can be so selective by voltage alone: electrolytic 10% oxalic acid outlines σ phase within about 6 seconds, carbides by 15–30 s, and grain boundaries only after 45–60 s at a fixed 6 V — the same reagent, reading out different constituents purely as a function of time and voltage. Lower it to 1–3 V and the response changes again.

11 · Safety, for the trainer

What you will need to teach, from the handbook’s own safety articles

This section is written to be taught from. It follows ASM Handbook Vol. 9’s dedicated article “Laboratory Safety in Metallography” (Vander Voort, p. 1081–1090) and the “Safety Precautions” and “Preparation and Handling of Etchants” parts of “Contrast Enhancement and Etching” (p. 294–312). It is a teaching summary, not a substitute for your lab’s own risk assessments or the current Safety Data Sheet for each chemical.

A · the four rules that prevent most incidents Order, ventilation, PPE, separation
  • Always add acid (or alkali) to water, slowly, while stirring — never the reverse. Mixing strong acids, NaOH/KOH or anhydrous AlCl₃ into water evolves large amounts of heat; incomplete mixing can layer and give “a delayed violent reaction” (p. 312).
  • Work under an exhaust hood. Mandatory for large quantities, macroetching, and any lengthy electropolishing (p. 312).
  • PPE every time: safety glasses or face shield, gloves, apron. Inspect gloves for holes — the handbook warns they “can impart a false sense of security” (p. 1082).
  • Store acids, bases, oxidants and flammable solvents separately, in fireproof cabinets. “Strong oxidants must not be stored along with acids, bases, or flammable solvents” (p. 1083).
B · the oxidizer + organic trap Nitric or perchloric acid with alcohol, glycerol or acetic anhydride

Most steel etchants mix an oxidizer (HNO₃) with an organic modifier (alcohol, glycerol). Mixing oxidizing agents with reducing agents “can result in violent or explosive reactions” (p. 312). Specifics the handbook names: nitric + glycerin/glycols can be explosive; glycerol should not be used with strong oxidizers (nitroglycerin can form); and the perchloric-acid + acetic-anhydride electropolishing mixtures are “exceptionally dangerous and should never be used” — the 1947 Los Angeles electroplating-factory accident killed 17 and injured 150 (p. 1085–1086).

C · picric acid An explosive when it dries out

“When picric acid is dry, it is a dangerous explosive… Picrates, which are metal salts of picric acid, are explosive. When picrates are dry, they can detonate readily, possibly spontaneously.” Buy small quantities, keep the crystals moist, store cool, and if it starts to dry, add water (p. 1087). Picral (the alcohol solution) can be stored safely, but “the etching residue should be discarded at the end of the day to avoid potential explosions” (p. 1087). It is incompatible with all oxidizable substances and with metals (forming picrates).

D · hydrofluoric acid Different from every other acid — treat it specially

HF “can cause painful and serious ulcers upon contacting the skin, unless washed off immediately” (p. 312). Burns can be deep and delayed; concentrated-HF exposure “requires immediate first aid and hospitalization” (p. 303). It needs HF-specific PPE, calcium-gluconate first aid on hand, and polyethylene/polypropylene containers (glass is attacked). It appears in Marshall’s-with-HF and in titanium etchants.

E · cyanide etchants Never near acid

Cyanide-containing etchants (e.g. the electrolytic NaCN etch for stainless σ phase, flagged “Dangerous!” in Table 8) can evolve hard-to-detect HCN gas from acidic solutions, and cyanide is absorbed through skin. Use an effective hood; render spent solution slightly alkaline with ammonia before flushing it away with copious water (p. 312). Keep cyanides and acids strictly apart.

F · the strong oxidant salts Permanganate, chromic acid, peroxide, bromine

“Potassium permanganate and sulfuric acid should never be mixed together, because a violent” reaction results (p. 1088) — relevant to Groesbeck’s and modified-Murakami reagents. 30% H₂O₂ is “as harmful… as… sulfuric acid” on skin (Marshall’s part B). Chromic acid (CrO₃ in water) is a powerful oxidant and a chronic-exposure hazard. Bromine, in deep-etch reagents, gives deep slow-healing burns and should be handled only by qualified staff.

First response, drilled before anyone etches

  • Chemicals on skin → wash off promptly with water and soap; seek medical attention for burns, especially over cuts (p. 312).
  • Chemicals in eyes → flush at once with large quantities of water and get medical help without delay.
  • A face-and-eye wash fountain and, where larger quantities are stored, a safety shower must be present, reachable, and tested on a schedule (p. 312).
  • Know the HF calcium-gluconate protocol separately — ordinary acid first aid is not enough for HF.

Before the session: paperwork & disposal

  • An MSDS/SDS must be on file and read for every chemical (OSHA Hazard Communication; ANSI 16-section format, p. 1081). Teach trainees to read the first-aid and incompatibility sections first.
  • Use vented or pressure-relief caps on stored etchants that can evolve gas (e.g. methanol–HNO₃), not sealed screw caps (p. 312).
  • Discard spent etchant promptly down a chemically resistant drain under the hood with copious running water; handle strongly acidic, alkaline or toxic solutions with extra care (p. 312).
  • Methanol is toxic through the skin; prefer ethanol at low HNO₃ concentrations, and use methanol only when the handbook calls for it (above ~5% HNO₃ the ethanol solution becomes unstable).

One sentence to open your training with, straight from the handbook: “All chemicals are potentially dangerous; formulating and using etchants requires thorough knowledge of the chemicals involved and the proper procedures for handling and mixing” (p. 312).
