Sympathetic nucleation: ferrite that nucleates on ferrite

A new ferrite crystal can form on the interface of one that already exists, even though the austenite there has been enriched in carbon. This page follows the problem from Aaronson and Wells (1956) through Shewmon’s ledge terraces and solute drag to in-situ microscopy, and shows where it decides whether an allotriomorph sprouts Widmanstätten plates.

What it is

A crystal of the same phase nucleates on the interphase boundary of an earlier one

Aaronson and Wells found groups of ferrite crystals in a 0.29C–0.76Mn–0.25Si steel that could only be explained if new crystals had nucleated on the α:γ boundaries of older ones. The 1995 overview tightened the definition: both crystals must differ in composition from the matrix throughout the transformation, which is what separates it from recrystallization-type stimulated nucleation.

“Sympathetic nucleation is defined as the nucleation of a precipitate crystal at an interphase boundary of a crystal of the same phase when these crystals differ in composition from their matrix phase throughout the transformation process.”[A1995] abstract, p. 107 (PDF 1)

Where it happens

AW ranked the sites: “Sympathetic nucleation takes place most frequently at intragranular plates, develops occasionally at the oriented-lattices boundaries of morphologies which nucleate at austenite grain boundaries, and occurs rarely, if ever, at the unoriented-lattices boundaries of the latter morphologies.” [AW1956] p. 1218 (PDF 3). The oriented-lattices boundary is the side of a grain-boundary crystal that faces the grain it holds an orientation relationship with.

Why it is not branching

Under KS with conjugate habit planes “only one {110}bcc plane can be parallel to a {111}fcc habit plane. Hence branching of a bcc precipitate plate formed in an fcc matrix or vice versa is crystallographically impossible” [A1995] p. 110 (PDF 4). Two plates on different {111}γ planes are two crystals, so a boundary must separate them, even when etching does not reveal it.

Why it matters for kinetics

Reynolds et al. attribute the bay and the incomplete reaction in Fe–C–Mo to it: “Extensive sympathetic nucleation of ferrite is observed at temperatures below that of the bay.” [R1990] p. 1433 (PDF 1). Most ferrite crystals of the first stage there are sympathetically nucleated (p. 1437).

The paradox

The austenite next to ferrite is the worst place to nucleate more ferrite

“The austenite adjacent to the austenite-ferrite boundaries will be greatly enriched in carbon, approximately to the value of the γ/(α + γ) equilibrium curve or its metastable extrapolation at the temperature of transformation. … Since rates of nucleation are considered to decrease exponentially with decreasing supersaturation, the frequency with which ferrite nuclei appear at austenite-ferrite boundaries should be negligible in relation to that at which they form in other regions of the austenite.”[AW1956] p. 1216 (PDF 1)

Read the carbon at the interface off the Ae3 line (extended below the eutectoid with cementite suppressed) at the hold temperature. AW wrote the volume driving force with a dilute expression, Eq. 8, ΔFv = −(RT/V) ln(X/Xγα), where X is the mole fraction of iron. Below it is written per mole, so no molar volume is needed. AW then let the interface austenite sit slightly below the Ae3 by ΔX (Eq. 9), because a crystal exactly on the line offers nothing.

A check against a modern database. AW quote “ln X/Xγα ≈ 0.21 in a 0.30 pct steel” at 550 °C, giving ΔFv = −48 cal cm⁻³ at austenite grain boundaries, and ln((Xγα+ΔX)/Xγα) ≈ 0.01, ΔFv = −2.3 cal cm⁻³ at the α:γ face with ΔX = 0.01 [AW1956] p. 1221 (PDF 6). Set 550 °C and 0.30 wt.% above: TCFE14 gives ln ≈ 0.09, about half of AW’s 0.21. Their extrapolation puts roughly 5 wt.% C at the interface, which matches their remark that the Brandt and Zener extrapolations “attain the composition of cementite” (p. 1222). Those extrapolation papers were not checked here.

The second objection

Even with enough driving force, a moving boundary destroys the embryo: “the continuously moving disordered α:β boundaries would overrun the embryos before they reach critical nucleus size, particularly during the early growth times of these boundaries” [A1995] p. 107 (PDF 1). AW already noted the same idea from Mehl and Dube for cementite embryos and concluded that slow plate faces should favour surviving nuclei [AW1956] p. 1222 (PDF 7).

The one term in its favour

“Only the free energy of activation for the diffusion of carbon in austenite, which decreases with increasing carbon content at a given temperature, is consistently favorable to sympathetic nucleation.” [AW1956] p. 1222 (PDF 7). It enters the pre-exponential factor, so it cannot rescue a barrier that has grown by the square of a driving-force ratio.

The paradox on Gibbs energy curves

A nucleus can only use the gap between the austenite tangent and the ferrite curve

The driving force for nucleating ferrite from austenite of composition x is found by the parallel-tangent construction: draw the tangent to Gγ at x, then find the ferrite composition where Gα has the same slope. The vertical gap between the two is the largest driving force any nucleus can have. Move the austenite composition from the bulk value toward the Ae3 and watch the gap close. On the Ae3 the tangent to austenite is the common tangent itself, so the gap is exactly zero. That is the paradox in one picture.

The nucleus

Replacing α:γ area with a low-energy α:α boundary pays for the lost supersaturation

AW used a hemispherical nucleus on a planar boundary (their Eqs. 2–7). At an austenite grain boundary the cap and the base are both α:γ, and the base destroys γ:γ area. At an α:γ face the base becomes an α:α boundary and destroys α:γ area. With E the strain energy:

ΔF*γγ = π(3σγα − σγγ)³ / [3(ΔFvγγ + E)²] (Eq. 4) ΔF*γα = π(σγα + σαα)³ / [3(ΔFvγα + E)²] (Eq. 7)

With AW’s inputs, σγγ = 850 erg cm⁻², σγα/σγγ = 0.74 and σγα/σαα = 0.71 [AW1956] p. 1221 (PDF 6), these reproduce their printed Eq. 10 (1.60 × 10⁻¹⁴ (cal cm⁻²)³; recomputed here 1.59) and Eq. 11 (5.01 × 10⁻¹⁴; recomputed 4.97), which confirms how Eqs. 4 and 7 read under the image. The ratio of the two barriers is a product of an interfacial factor and a driving-force factor Φ = ΔGsym/ΔGbulk.

Why the base differs my reading The nucleus is a crystal of ferrite, and every surface of it touches something. At an austenite grain boundary both neighbours are austenite crystals, so wherever the nucleus touches either grain it is a ferrite:austenite interface, the cap against grain 1 and the base against grain 2. The base has no ferrite to join, so it keeps the α:γ energy (and it destroys a patch of γ:γ boundary, which pays back some energy). At an α:γ interface the base touches an existing ferrite crystal, not austenite. The new crystal and the old one are both ferrite, so the base is an α:α boundary, whose energy is low and falls towards zero as the two orientations approach each other. That swap, a costly α:γ base for a cheap α:α base, is the whole gain of sympathetic nucleation in Eq. 7, and why σαα appears there in place of σγα in the base term. The page’s Eqs. 4 and 7 are AW’s; the cap and base split above is from the text description around them, since the equations themselves are images in the PDF.

Reading Eqs. 4 and 7 my derivation

Both equations are the same calculation with a different bookkeeping of the base. Take a hemisphere of radius r. Its volume is (2/3)πr³, so the bulk term is (2/3)πr³(ΔFv + E), negative because ΔFv is. Its curved cap has area 2πr² and costs σγα per unit area. The flat base has area πr² and is where the two sites differ:

grain boundary: cap 2πr²σγα + base πr²σγα − πr²σγγ = πr²(3σγα − σγγ)
α:γ face: cap 2πr²σγα + base πr²σαα − πr²σγα = πr²(σγα + σαα)

Call that bracket Σ, the net interfacial energy per πr². Then ΔF(r) = (2π/3)r³(ΔFv + E) + πr²Σ. The first term falls with r and the second rises, so ΔF has a maximum. Setting dΔF/dr = 0 gives the critical radius r* = Σ / |ΔFv + E|, and substituting back gives ΔF* = πΣ³ / [3(ΔFv + E)²], which is Eq. 4 with Σ = 3σγα − σγγ and Eq. 7 with Σ = σγα + σαα. This reproduces the printed form, which supports reading the grain-boundary base as α:γ.

What it says

The barrier grows as the cube of the interfacial energy and falls as the square of the net driving force. Interfaces cost; driving force pays. Because the exponents differ, a modest loss of driving force hurts more than the same gain in interfacial energy helps. Halving ΔFv quadruples the barrier; halving Σ divides it by eight.

Why it matters here

Nucleation rate goes as exp(−ΔF*/kT), so a barrier ratio is an exponent, not a small correction. At the face, Σ shrinks (σαα is below σγα, so Σ can be below the grain-boundary value) but ΔFv also shrinks, since the austenite there sits near the Ae3. The ratio below is that contest: the interfacial factor [(σγα + σαα)/(3σγα − σγγ)]³ against the driving-force factor 1/Φ², with E set to zero.

What AW concluded

The interfacial deterrent disappears where α:γ area “with average specific interfacial free energies” is replaced “by ferrite-ferrite boundaries with energies equal to or less than 0.72 of the average grain boundary energy in randomly polycrystalline ferrite” [AW1956] p. 1221 (PDF 6). The supersaturation deficit cannot be removed the same way; AW leaned on it shrinking at low temperature and on the large α:γ area in coarse-grained steel (p. 1222).

A number that does not reproduce yet my arithmetic

Setting the interfacial factors of Eqs. 4 and 7 equal gives σαα = 2σγα − σγγ = 0.65σγα, which is 0.46 of AW’s average σαα. AW print 0.98 (Eq. 13) and 0.72, and 0.72 = 0.71/0.98, so their Eq. 13 is σγα/σαα,crit = 0.98. Eqs. 12–13 are images in the PDF. How 0.98 follows is unresolved here; the map uses the equal-factor line.

The pillbox version

Menon and Aaronson’s pillbox nucleus, bounded by low-energy faces, gives a stronger result: when γαα/σα:β,c is below about 0.2, “ΔGs* = 1/3 ΔGh* even when the net driving force for sympathetic nucleation is only about half that for homogeneous nucleation” [A1995] p. 115 (PDF 9). The same review warns that back-calculated site densities come out orders of magnitude too low (p. 114).

Because the allotriomorph “rarely exhibits an exact OR to either parent”, reaching a fast-growth orientation “inevitably requires some orientation change” (p. 132).

Shewmon’s answer

A boundary that stops lets carbon leave, and the supersaturation comes back

Shewmon (1965) moved the problem from disordered, continuously moving boundaries to ledged ones: “the immobile terraces of growth ledges at partially or fully coherent interphase boundaries would provide a lesser contribution of interfacial energy but a much larger supersaturation through which to drive the nucleation process. The immobility of these terraces permits solute to flow away from or toward them after the passage of a growth ledge, thereby restoring at least some of the original supersaturation” [A1995] p. 108 (PDF 2). Kinks on ledge risers move fastest and are the worst sites (p. 117).

For a spike of width l = D/v left against a face that has stopped, the excess decays as (xg − x∞)/(xg0 − x∞) = erfcx(√(t/τ)) with τ = D/v². Half is gone at 0.59τ and 90 % at 31.5τ, so recovery is a slow power law (exact solution for constant D and semi-infinite austenite, derivation not shown). It is generic, not tied to an alloy.

This is why a ledged face can host sympathetic nucleation while a planar face of the same alloy cannot: the terrace waits, the riser moves.

The split treatment raises the ratio because “a nearly instantaneous increase in supersaturation in the matrix in contact with the interphase boundaries attends the abrupt decrease in transformation temperature” (p. 119).

Reynolds et al. add solute drag. In Fe–C–Mo below the bay, a slower α:γ boundary means terraces “survive” longer before the next riser overruns them: “The supersaturation for ferrite formation at the terraces then increases to the level where sympathetic nucleation can occur again.” These evasions of the solute-drag-like effect repeat until the carbon in the remaining austenite stops them, and “transformation stasis occurs when the SDLE is strong enough to produce growth stasis of the individual sympathetically nucleated ferrite crystals” and sympathetic nucleation becomes negligible [R1990] p. 1454 (PDF 22).

Evidence

A single section cannot prove it, so the evidence has moved to in-situ microscopy and EBSD

AW’s rules of evidence for one plane of polish: the new crystal does not touch a matrix grain boundary or subboundary; shape, size and position rule out a hidden common origin at a grain boundary; the α:α boundary was not made by polygonization or migration; intragranular nucleation rates are low; and the amount transformed is small [AW1956] pp. 1216–1217 (PDF 1–2). The 1995 review states the lesson plainly:

“a configuration of crystals cannot be considered to have been created by sympathetic nucleation unless independent nucleation of all crystals involved at separate sites coupled with impingement and a deceptive appearance generated by a non-representative plane of polish can be safely considered to be highly improbable.”[A1995] p. 109 (PDF 3)

Testing it

Sympathetic nucleation is one of the routes from allotriomorph to Widmanstätten plate

The Widmanstätten lesson sets it beside interface instability and kinetic-branch change. These measurements can tell which route operates in a given steel and temperature.

In-situ heating EBSD

Measure the misorientation at sideplate roots. Instability predicts none; sympathetic nucleation predicts a low-angle boundary, 4–12° in the confocal work. Look for two-step profiles, and for how far the allotriomorph sits from the KS variant expected for the plate.

HT confocal microscopy

Watch whether plates start at steps or facets on the allotriomorph, as Phelan and Dippenaar saw, and whether new plates appear on existing faces after a temperature drop.

Split isothermal holds

A drop from T₁ to T₂ raises the supersaturation at existing faces for a while [A1995] p. 119. In a dilatometer, compare the kinetics after the drop with an isothermal hold at T₂, then check the quenched sample by EBSD for new crystals on old faces.

Plain carbon against Mn or Mo steels

If solute drag slows the face enough for supersaturation to return, as Reynolds et al. argued for Fe–C–Mo, sympathetic sideplates should be commoner in the alloyed steel at the same undercooling.

Temperature

AW saw sympathetic nucleation rates rise as temperature falls, mainly at 550–475 °C, and the dominant configuration change from edge-to-face to face-to-face below about 525 °C (pp. 1220–1222).

Open questions

What is still unresolved

  • AW Eq. 13 (0.98). Equal interfacial factors give σαα = 0.65σγα; AW print σγα/σαα,crit = 0.98 and the 0.72 that follows.
  • AW’s extrapolated Ae3. ln X/Xγα ≈ 0.21 at 550 °C is about twice the TCFE14 value. Brandt (1946) and Zener (1946) are not in the library.
  • Why does edge-to-face replace face-to-face at high driving force? Face-to-face has the lower interfacial cost and the same driving force at a given site, yet loses in lower bainite. [A1995] pp. 120–121 leave it open; AW also had no explanation for the frequency of sheaves (p. 1222).
  • Why the misorientation steps? Phelan et al. suggest successive nuclei keep a common pole to lower σαα; there are few data for boundary energies below 10° or their temperature dependence ([P2005] p. 133).
