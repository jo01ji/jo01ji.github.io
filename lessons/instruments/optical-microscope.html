The metallurgical light microscope

A polished and etched metal is an opaque, almost perfect mirror. Every bit of contrast you see in a micrograph comes from how that mirror sends light back into the objective: some areas reflect less, some are tilted, some are rough on a scale finer than the wavelength. This lesson takes an established reflected-light microscope apart, upright and inverted, and lets you drive a simulated one on four synthetic metallographic samples. The parts are covered one by one: lamp, diaphragms, beamsplitter, objective, tube lens and camera. Each section shows how that part changes what you see.

1 · What the instrument has to do

A polished metal is an opaque mirror, so all the contrast comes from reflection

A biological microscope shines light through a thin, transparent section. A metal stays opaque even when ground to about 30 µm, so a metallographic microscope lights the specimen from the same side it looks at it (Evident, ref. 1). The objective works twice: it is the condenser on the way in and the imaging lens on the way out. Four facts about metallographic specimens drive the whole design.

Opaque

No transmitted light, no condenser under the stage. The light enters through a vertical illuminator and a beamsplitter above the objective.

Reflectivity contrast

Phases and inclusions reflect different fractions of the light. MnS and oxides look grey or dark against the matrix even when unetched.

Slope contrast

Etching grooves grain boundaries and tilts facets. A facet tilted by θ sends its reflection off by 2θ. If that is outside the objective's acceptance cone, the facet looks dark.

No cover glass, and flat

Metallurgical objectives are corrected for an uncovered surface. The polished face must also be flat over the field, because the depth of field at high magnification is under a micrometre.

The specimens are synthetic: a procedural surface (grains, grooves, facets, lamellae, inclusions, scratches, relief) rendered with a geometric-optics model of reflection into the objective cone. The brightness is auto-exposed, as a camera would be.

2 · The light path, part by part

Light enters from the side, turns through the objective onto the specimen, and comes back through the beamsplitter

Every reflected-light stand has the same chain. A lamp and collector feed a vertical illuminator with two irises. A beamsplitter at 45° turns the beam into the objective. The objective lights the specimen and collects the reflection. The returning light passes straight through the beamsplitter to a tube lens, which forms the intermediate image for the eyepieces and camera. Click a part on the drawing or in the list.

3 · Key concept: Köhler illumination, and critical illumination for contrast

Two interleaved sets of planes: one images the lamp, the other images the specimen

Köhler illumination is the arrangement every good microscope uses. The lamp is never focused on the specimen. Instead the collector focuses the lamp onto the aperture diaphragm, and the illuminator lens relays that image to the objective's back focal plane. Each point of the lamp therefore lights the whole field with a parallel bundle, which is why the field stays even with a coiled filament (ref. 2). The field diaphragm is focused on the specimen, and the specimen in turn on the camera sensor and the eyepiece field stop. The drawing unfolds the path at the specimen, which acts as a mirror, so the two passes through the objective sit side by side.

The older alternative is critical (Nelson) illumination: the collector focuses the lamp straight onto the specimen, here by way of the field diaphragm plane, which is conjugate with it. It is simpler and very bright, but the image of the lamp is superimposed on the image of the specimen, so a coiled filament or an LED die shows up as uneven bands and patches (ref. 7). Switch between the two below and watch the ray diagram, the back focal plane and the small eyepiece view change together.

Looking at the back focal plane

Pull out an eyepiece (or use a Bertrand lens) and you see the objective's back focal plane: a bright disc, the lamp image, inside the dark rim of the objective pupil. The aperture diaphragm sets how much of the pupil the lamp fills. Evident recommends 60 to 95 % open, smaller for highly reflective specimens to cut glare (ref. 2). In reflected light the objective is its own condenser, so one aperture setting works across objectives.

In dark field the light arrives through the outer ring of a BD objective, outside the pupil. In oblique light it is a small disc pushed to one side. A flat mirror sends each illumination direction back to the opposite side of the pupil.

Critical illumination works if the source is itself uniform: a frosted bulb, a diffuser, or the face of a liquid light guide. A diffuser in a Köhler path does the same job at the cost of brightness. For quantitative work on intensity, such as image analysis of phase fractions or DRM reflectance, Köhler plus a flat-field correction is the safe choice.

Setting up Köhler on a metallograph

  1. Focus a flat, polished specimen with a mid-power objective.
  2. Close the field diaphragm until its leaves appear. Centre them with the illuminator's centring screws, then open it until it sits just outside the field of view (ref. 2).
  3. Open the aperture diaphragm fully, then close it slowly while watching contrast. Stop where the image gains contrast without becoming grainy; check the back focal plane if unsure.
  4. For photomicrographs, open the field diaphragm only enough to clear the camera frame.
  5. Use the green filter for monochrome cameras and for visual work on high-NA objectives: objectives are best corrected near the middle of the spectrum.

What the two irises do and do not do

  • The aperture diaphragm controls contrast, resolution and depth of field. Using it to dim the image throws resolution away; use the lamp intensity or a neutral density filter.
  • The field diaphragm controls glare. Light sent to parts of the specimen outside the field still comes back through the objective and fogs the image.
  • Neither changes the magnification.

Try it on the ferrite–pearlite specimen at 100×. With the aperture fully open the finest pearlite colonies resolve into lamellae. Close it below about 30 % and they collapse into dark, unresolved patches, because the first diffraction orders of the lamellae no longer reach the pupil.

4 · The objective

NA sets resolution, depth of field and how steep a facet can be and still look bright

The numerical aperture NA = n·sin α, where α is the half-angle of the cone the objective accepts and n is the refractive index between the lens and the specimen (1 for air). It is the single most important number on the barrel.

resolution d = 1.22 λ / (NAobj + NAill) → 0.61 λ / NA when the illumination fills the objective (ref. 3)
depth of field ≈ λ n / NA² + n e / (M · NA) (e = smallest distance the detector resolves, M = magnification at the detector; ref. 7)

The instruments on this page use the partially coherent form d = λ/(NAobj + NAill) for the finest periodic detail, such as pearlite lamellae: a grating is resolved once its first diffraction order enters the pupil for at least part of the illumination. That form makes the role of the aperture diaphragm explicit. The two forms differ by a constant factor and agree on every trend.

With the aperture closed, transfer stays at 1 for coarse detail and then drops sharply, as in coherent imaging. With it fully open, the cut-off moves to finer spacings, but mid-range contrast falls. Blue light improves resolution by about 18 % over green. That matters for fine pearlite and bainite, but most objectives are corrected best in the green. Magnification beyond about 1000 NA is "empty": the image gets bigger but no new detail appears.

5 · The beamsplitter

A 50:50 half-mirror sends at most a quarter of the lamp light to the camera

The beamsplitter has to reflect lamp light toward the objective and then transmit the returning light toward the tube lens. With reflectance R and transmittance T = 1 − R (no absorption), the fraction that reaches the camera is R·T·Rs, where Rs is the specimen reflectance. R·T peaks at 0.25 when R = 0.5, so even a perfect mirror specimen returns only a quarter of the lamp light. Reflected-light microscopes are bright anyway because metals reflect strongly.

Plate, cube, ring mirror

  • Plate. A partially silvered plane glass at 45°, silvered on the side facing the lamp and anti-reflection coated on the side facing the tube (ref. 1). Cheap and light; its second surface can add a faint ghost.
  • Cube. Two cemented prisms with the coating on the diagonal. No ghost from a second surface and no beam offset, but its faces must be anti-reflection coated or they send lamp light straight back to the camera.
  • Dark-field mirror. A fully silvered mirror with an elliptical central hole (ref. 1). Lamp light reflects off the silvered ring into the outer channel of a BD objective, and the returning image passes through the hole.
  • Light trap. The (1 − R) of lamp light that passes straight through the beamsplitter has to be absorbed. If it reflects off the inside of the housing it comes back as glare.
  • Polarising cube + λ/4 plate. Sends up to half of an unpolarised lamp's light to the camera, which is why laser confocals use it. It is itself a polariser pair, so it changes polarised-light and DIC contrast. Keep it out of a general-purpose metallograph.
6 · Contrast modes

Every contrast mode is a different rule for which reflected rays the objective accepts

Take one small facet of the specimen, tilted by θ. A mirror sends the reflected cone off by 2θ. Bright field lights the facet along the axis and collects whatever comes back inside the acceptance cone, so flat areas are bright and steep grooves dark. Dark field lights it from outside the cone, so flat areas send everything back outside and look black, while grooves, scratches and pits tip some of the light into the objective and glow. Oblique light leans the incoming beam to one side, so facets that face it look bright and the others dark, like low sun on a landscape. Polarised light and DIC keep the bright-field geometry and add a polariser and analyser, and for DIC a Nomarski prism.

Cubic metals (ferrite, austenite, Al, Cu, Ni) are optically isotropic. Under crossed polars a polished steel stays dark however you turn the stage, so polarised light cannot give grain orientation in steel the way it does in titanium. Switch the specimen to α-titanium and turn the stage to see the difference. For orientations in steel you need EBSD, or an etch that produces crystallographic facets that oblique or directional illumination can read.

7 · Eyepieces, camera and magnification

The tube lens makes one real image; eyepieces and camera only look at it

The intermediate image sits at the eyepiece field stop and, through the camera adapter, on the sensor. The eyepiece (usually 10×) magnifies it for the eye. The camera does not care about eyepiece magnification: what matters is the pixel size projected back onto the specimen, which is the sensor pixel divided by the objective magnification times the adapter magnification.

Choosing the adapter

A lower adapter magnification shows more of the eyepiece field but makes each pixel bigger on the specimen. At low objective magnification a 0.5× adapter usually still samples well enough. At 100× it throws nothing away, because the optics are the limit. At 10× to 20×, check the resolution/pixel ratio: big pixels can undersample the fine detail the objective delivers, and the camera then shows less than the eye.

A monochrome camera with the green filter usually gives the sharpest images. A colour sensor's Bayer filter spreads each colour over two to four pixels, which lowers the effective sampling.

9 · Links to ferrite and martensite work

Where these modes meet transformation studies

  • Surface relief. A displacive product forming on a pre-polished surface tilts the surface. That is the oldest evidence for an invariant-plane strain in martensite, and the same test is used on Widmanstätten ferrite and bainite. Bright field barely shows it, because the tilts are a few degrees. Oblique light, DIC and interferometry show it clearly. Try the martensite-relief specimen in bright field, then in DIC.
  • Allotriomorphic versus Widmanstätten ferrite. Nital-etched sections separate them by shape and by where they sit. Grain-boundary allotriomorphs line the prior austenite boundaries, and sideplates grow into the grain. The grooves at the ferrite/pearlite boundaries are the slope contrast of section 6.
  • In-situ high-temperature confocal. Laser scanning confocal microscopes for in-situ heating are reflected-light instruments. They see prior austenite grain boundaries through thermal grooving and transformations through surface relief, which is the same slope contrast as here.
  • Pearlite spacing. Interlamellar spacings close to the resolution limit are a classic reason to switch to SEM. Section 4 shows where the light-optical cut-off sits for each objective.
  • Orientation. Steel is optically isotropic, so orientations come from EBSD. Directional reflectance microscopy, which reads etch-facet orientation from oblique illumination at many angles, is a light-optical alternative. A later lesson will build on the oblique mode here.
10 · Other key concepts
Conjugate planes

Planes that are images of one another. The aperture set (lamp, aperture diaphragm, objective back focal plane, exit pupil) and the field set (field diaphragm, specimen, intermediate image, sensor) alternate along the path.

Back focal plane

The plane behind the objective where parallel bundles from the specimen come to a focus. Each point in it is one direction at the specimen. Its rim is the NA.

Infinity space

The stretch between an infinity-corrected objective and the tube lens where light from each specimen point is parallel. Beamsplitters, polarisers and prisms can sit here without blurring the image.

Field number

The diameter in mm of the eyepiece field stop. Field of view on the specimen = field number / objective magnification.

Parfocal distance

Distance from the objective's mounting shoulder to the specimen in focus. Objectives of one maker share it, so the specimen stays nearly in focus when you change objective.

Working distance

Free space between the front lens and the specimen in focus. It shrinks as NA rises. Long working distance objectives trade NA for space.

Partial coherence

Illumination that comes from a range of angles set by the aperture diaphragm. A small aperture is nearly coherent: more contrast and depth of field, less resolution and more diffraction fringes.

Glare (flare)

Stray light that adds a uniform veil and lowers contrast. Sources: an open field diaphragm, dirty or uncoated surfaces, an untrapped beamsplitter.
