The FDM polymer 3D printer

A fused deposition modelling (FDM, also called fused filament fabrication, FFF) printer is a hot nozzle on a motion system. A motor pushes a 1.75 mm plastic filament into a heated block, the melt leaves a nozzle about 0.4 mm wide, and the machine draws the part one layer at a time. Almost everything that separates a slow, wobbly printer from a fast, accurate one comes down to two questions: what has to move, and how stiffly. This lesson takes the machine apart, animates the main families (bed slingers, CoreXY, H-bot, CoreXZ, delta, belt printers, IDEX and toolchangers) and then goes through what owners typically modify, using the open-source Voron printers as a running example of a machine built to be modified.

1 · What the machine has to do

Melt a filament at a controlled rate and place it where the slicer says, layer after layer

A slicer turns a 3D model into G-code: a long list of straight moves, each with a target position and an amount of filament to push. The printer's firmware (Marlin, RepRapFirmware or Klipper) turns those moves into step pulses for the motors. Four jobs decide the design.

Metering the melt

The extruder pushes filament at a set rate. Plastic is viscoelastic and compressible in the hot zone, so the flow at the nozzle lags the push. Pressure advance in the firmware corrects this (section 5).

Moving the nozzle

Two or three axes position the nozzle relative to the bed. Which parts move, and how heavy they are, sets how hard the machine can accelerate before it rings (sections 3 and 4).

Bonding the layers

Each new road must weld to the one below while it is still hot enough for the polymer chains to diffuse across the interface. This is a sintering and interdiffusion problem (section 6).

Keeping the part still

The first layer must stick, and the part must not warp as it cools. A heated bed, a build surface (PEI, textured or smooth) and, for ABS, ASA, PC and nylon, an enclosure do this (ref. 8).

2 · The toolhead, part by part

Cold at the top, molten at the bottom, with a sharp break in between

The hotend has to keep the filament stiff until the last few millimetres, then melt it fast. The heat break is a thin tube that limits heat flowing upward, and the heat sink and its fan keep everything above it cold. If heat creeps upward, filament softens in the wrong place and jams; this is called heat creep. Click a part on the drawing or in the list.

The CoreXY equations

Both motors drive belts that end on the toolhead. If motor A pays out belt length ΔA and motor B pays out ΔB, the toolhead moves

ΔX = ½ (ΔA + ΔB), ΔY = ½ (ΔA − ΔB)

so a pure X move turns both motors the same way, a pure Y move turns them opposite ways, and a 45° diagonal turns only one motor (ref. 1). Watch the two motor dials in the animation.

Why CoreXY is faster in practice

The motors are the heaviest part of the motion system. In CoreXY they sit on the frame, so the X axis only moves the toolhead, and the Y axis moves the toolhead plus the gantry beam. In a bed slinger the Y motor has to move the bed, its heater, the build plate and the growing part. Less mass at the same belt stiffness means a higher resonance frequency (section 4), so the input shaper smooths less and the machine can accelerate harder.

4 · Key concept: moving mass, resonance and input shaping

A belt-driven axis is a mass on a spring; every corner rings it

A belt behaves like a spring of stiffness k, and the toolhead, gantry or bed is the mass m. A sudden change in acceleration at a corner sets it oscillating at the natural frequency f = (1/2π)·√(k/m), and the nozzle draws that oscillation into the wall as ripples. This is ringing or ghosting. The ripples are spaced v/f apart, where v is the print speed, which is how Klipper's test print measures f (ref. 3). The amplitude scales with acceleration divided by ω², so heavy, soft axes ring more.

Input shaping cancels the oscillation by splitting each acceleration step into two or three smaller steps, timed half a period apart, so the second excites a wave that cancels the first. The cost is a small rounding (smoothing) of corners, which grows as f falls (ref. 3). The model below integrates the mass–spring equation for the start of a straight wall just after a corner.

On a Klipper printer, an ADXL345 accelerometer and Klipper's SHAPER_CALIBRATE measure f directly (ref. 3). Shaper impulse timings follow Klipper's shaper definitions (ref. 4). Klipper's default damping ratio is 0.1.

Two quotes from the Klipper documentation explain the architecture trade-off. On bed slingers, "as filament accumulates on the moving bed, the resonance frequency will decrease", so a more tolerant shaper such as EI is suggested; move the printed-part slider to see it. And below about 20 to 25 Hz, they suggest stiffening the printer or reducing the moving mass, because no shaper can then avoid heavy smoothing (ref. 3).

5 · Extrusion: the bead, volumetric flow and pressure advance

Speed is limited by how fast the hotend can melt plastic, not only by the motion system

The slicer models each extruded road as a rectangle with semicircular ends, of width w and height h (ref. 6). Its cross-section is A = (w − h)·h + π h²/4, and the volumetric flow the hotend must supply is Q = A·v. Every hotend has a maximum flow above which the filament cannot heat through in the time it spends in the melt zone. You measure it with a flow test that ramps speed until extrusion fails (ref. 7).

Pressure advance

The melt between the drive gears and the nozzle tip acts like a spring: to raise the flow you must first compress it, and when you slow down it keeps oozing. At a corner the printer brakes, so the corner bulges and the start of the next line is thin. Pressure advance "accounts for pressure by pushing in extra filament during acceleration and retracting that filament during deceleration" (ref. 5). Klipper adds an extruder lead equal to K times the extrusion rate, so the right K equals the melt's response time τ in this first-order model. Typical K values are between 0.050 and 1.000, with the high end used by Bowden setups, where the long tube adds compliance (ref. 5).

The lag is modelled as a single time constant τ; real melts are non-linear. τ here is illustrative. Set K equal to τ to cancel the error.

6 · Key concept: how the layers weld, and why it is a metallurgy problem

Each new road sinters onto the one below while it is hot, then the chains have to diffuse across

Bond formation between two roads has two stages. First the two touching cylinders coalesce under surface tension, growing a neck, like two powder particles sintering. Sun, Rizvi, Bellehumeur and Gu modelled this with a Frenkel-type viscous sintering law and found that for ABS P400 "the neck growth becomes negligible at temperatures below 200°C", so it happens only in the short time after deposition (refs. 15, 17). Second, polymer chains diffuse across the interface and entangle, like Wool and O'Connor's crack healing by reptation (ref. 20). Seppala and co-workers at NIST combined infrared imaging, rheology and fracture tests and condensed the changing temperature into an equivalent isothermal weld time (ref. 16).

Every number is illustrative: you choose the threshold, cooling constant and sintering time scale. The contact temperature uses the result for two bodies of the same material touching. Frenkel's law for two equal spheres or cylinders is (x/a)² ≈ (3/2)·Γt/(ηa) (ref. 14), counted here only while the interface is above the threshold. The point is the trend: a hotter nozzle, a warm chamber, slower part cooling or shorter layer times all buy welding time.

Links to physical metallurgy

  • Sintering. Frenkel's viscous neck growth is the amorphous-material case of the same two-particle model used for metal powders, where the transport is by diffusion rather than viscous flow (Kuczynski). Powder metallurgy and polymer FDM share the neck-ratio language.
  • Non-isothermal kinetics. The equivalent isothermal weld time is the additivity rule you use to turn a continuous-cooling path into isothermal transformation kinetics, or the tempering parameter in steels.
  • Intrinsic heat treatment. Each new layer reheats the ones below, as in laser powder bed fusion. In metal AM it tempers martensite or ages precipitates; here it extends welding and, in semicrystalline polymers, lets crystals grow.
  • Crystallisation. PLA, PA and PP crystallise by nucleation and growth, described with the same Avrami (JMAK) kinetics as ferrite. Annealing a PLA part after printing raises its crystallinity and heat resistance, and it also shrinks and can warp it.
  • Anisotropy. Parts are weakest across the layers (Z), because the interfaces are the weak links. That is the polymer cousin of columnar grains and texture in metal AM.

7 · Multi-material: swap the filament, or swap the nozzle

A single nozzle must purge on every change; IDEX and toolchangers do not

There are four ways to print more than one material. A filament changer (Bambu AMS, Prusa MMU, ERCF, Box Turtle) feeds several spools into one nozzle, so every change has to flush the old colour into a purge tower or out of a chute. Two nozzles on one carriage avoid purging but carry both hotends everywhere. IDEX gives each nozzle its own X carriage, so the idle one parks off the part. A toolchanger keeps complete toolheads in docks and picks one up when needed. The Prusa XL page puts the difference bluntly: "filament-switching solutions waste dozens of seconds on purging and reloading" (ref. 9).

9 · Example: the open-source Voron printers

A family of printers designed from the start to be built, tuned and modified

Voron Design publishes complete open-source printers: frames, printed parts, wiring and Klipper configurations. Builders source the parts or buy kits, print the plastic parts themselves, and then modify them, which is why so many of the mods in section 8 started in the Voron community (ref. 18). Most models are enclosed CoreXY machines, so they already have what many bed-slinger upgrades try to add.

  • Firmware. Voron printers run Klipper, so input shaping, pressure advance and macros are configuration changes, not firmware rebuilds.
  • Toolheads. The toolhead's hotend and extruder set the flow limit of section 5; the larger Vorons use the Stealthburner and the V0 a smaller version of it.
  • Enclosure. Most models are enclosed, so ABS and ASA print well. A carbon filter such as a Nevermore is the usual first addition, because ABS and ASA give off fumes and particles.
  • Good first steps on any Klipper printer. An accelerometer run for input shaping, a pressure advance calibration, a max-flow test, a chamber thermistor and, for an enclosed printer, a filter. All are cheap and each corresponds to a section of this page.

In a materials lab, even a small printer is enough for sample holders, polishing jigs, mounts and instrument parts. Print anything that sits near a hot stage or furnace in ASA or PC rather than PLA, and use the material guide above for the limits. A separate lesson covers the Voron 0.2 in detail.
